## Landmark layer (pre-2010) — the foundations the docs never name

### 1. Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 671–684. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80001-X
- **What it is:** the origin of "depth of processing" — memory is a by-product of *what you do* with information (structural → phonemic → semantic), not a storage container. Deeper, more elaborate processing → more durable memory.
- **Why it's a gap:** memory-techniques' first strategy is literally titled "Increase complexity" and teaches "actively mold it, re-explain in your own words, connect to prior knowledge" — that *is* levels of processing, but the doc never cites the paper that proved it. The "myth vs reality" callout ("whether you *handled* it right determines whether it's stored") is also a restatement.
- `improves → memory` (the six-strategies section: Increase complexity, Handle it immediately, Pause for handling)

### 2. Baddeley, A. D., & Hitch, G. (1974). Working memory. In G. H. Bower (Ed.), *The Psychology of Learning and Motivation*, Vol. 8, pp. 47–89. Academic Press. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0079-7421(08)60452-1
- **What it is:** the working-memory model — a limited-capacity workspace with separable components, replacing the idea of a single short-term "store."
- **Why it's a gap:** memory-techniques' whole "how memory actually works" table (workbench metaphor, weight limit, 15–30 s window, interference) is the Baddeley model in plain language — the doc cites Miller and Cowan for capacity but never the model's origin. The "weight limit" callout's "an item is fuzzy" point (patterns/prior knowledge lighten the load) is the model's chunking mechanism.
- `improves → memory` (how-memory-works section + weight-limit callout)

### 3. Tulving, E., & Thomson, D. M. (1973). Encoding specificity and retrieval processes in episodic memory. *Psychological Review*, 80(5), 352–373. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0020071
- **What it is:** retrieval depends on the match between encoding cues and retrieval cues — what you encoded determines what you can later get out, and with what prompts.
- **Why it's a gap:** learning-system teaches "match the method to how you'll actually use the knowledge" and "match your cues to reality: flashcard cues don't transfer to being asked a complex question" — that's encoding specificity verbatim, uncited. The retrieval-method spectrum (recognition → cued → free recall) is the same principle.
- `improves → system` (retrieval-method section), `memory` (practice retrieval — "match your cues to reality" line)

### 4. Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- **What it is:** the modern origin of the testing effect — taking a test improves long-term retention more than re-studying, even with no feedback.
- **Why it's a gap:** the shelf cites the *derivatives* (Karpicke & Blunt 2011; Rowland 2014; Adesope 2017; Agarwal 2021) but never the 2006 paper that re-launched the field. study-advice's evidence callout says "the test effect is one of the most replicated results" — the replication story starts here.
- `improves → study` (evidence callout), `memory` (retrieval-practice callout), `orders`

### 5. Flavell, J. H. (1979). Metacognition and cognitive monitoring: A new area of cognitive-developmental inquiry. *American Psychologist*, 34(10), 906–911. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.34.10.906
- **What it is:** the founding paper of metacognition — metacognitive knowledge (about persons, tasks, strategies) + metacognitive experiences; monitoring and control as the two jobs.
- **Why it's a gap:** metacognition-and-calibration's opening section is titled "Monitoring and control — the two jobs" — Flavell's exact framing — and the doc never cites him. The doc is otherwise well-cited (Koriat, Dunning, Nisbett & Wilson); the field's founder is the missing anchor.
- `improves → metacog` (monitoring-control section)

### 6. Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. *Psychological Bulletin*, 133(1), 65–94. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65
- **What it is:** the definitive procrastination meta-analysis (691 correlations / 216 studies) + temporal motivation theory — task aversiveness, delay, and expectancy drive procrastination.
- **Why it's a gap:** self-management covers procrastination (Eisenhower, 2-minute rule, Parkinson) with **zero** citations for the phenomenon itself. The doc's "why we procrastinate" framing (aversive task, distant reward) is Steel's model.
- `improves → self` (procrastination section)

### 7. Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. *Review of Educational Research*, 77(1), 81–112. https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487 — *(already cited in skill-acquisition)*

## Recent layer (2010–2026) — extensions and corrections

### 8. Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020). The power of feedback revisited: A meta-analysis of educational feedback research. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, Article 3087. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087
- **What it is:** the 2020 update of the feedback evidence — ~2,000 effect sizes from 434 studies; feedback is powerful (d ≈ 0.55 overall) *and* highly variable: what matters is the *kind* of feedback (task-level and process-level beat praise/punishment), not its presence.
- **Why it's a gap:** skill-acquisition cites Hattie & Timperley 2007 (added in the last roadmap pass) but not the field's current meta. The "direction of feedback" nuance (praise ≈ 0.1, task cues ≈ 0.4–0.8) sharpens the doc's feedback callout.
- `improves → skill` (feedback callout)

### 9. Hagger, M. S., et al. (2016). A multilab preregistered replication of the ego-depletion effect. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 11(4), 546–573. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873
- **What it is:** a 23-lab, N = 2,141 preregistered replication of the two-task ego-depletion paradigm. Result: **the depletion effect essentially vanishes** (d ≈ 0.04, CI excludes the original meta-analytic estimate). The "limited resource" framing is contested.
- **Why it's a gap (contradiction — the research equivalent of a bug):** wanting-vs-liking §sec-urge currently states, as established fact: *"Suppressing urges drains self-control (suppression produces ego depletion) — a depleted system loses to the urge exactly when it is weakest."* The replication record says the *resource* mechanism is not robust. **This does not kill the section's argument** — the suppression-backfire case stands on rebound (Wegner; Wang meta), reactance (Brehm), and AVE (Marlatt & Gordon) — but the *mechanism* must be stated honestly: the cost of suppression is rebound + raised value + rigid-rule bingeing, not a depletable resource. Fix the sentence in both twins.
- `improves → wanting` (sec-urge, the "Suppression is expensive" bullet)

### 10. Zimmerman, B. J. (2002). Becoming a self-regulated learner: An overview. *Theory Into Practice*, 41(2), 64–70. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2
- **What it is:** the canonical self-regulated-learning framework — forethought → performance → self-reflection cycle; self-observation and self-recording as the engine of improvement.
- **Why it's a gap:** study-advice's "keep a learning log" lesson (and the whole learning-records/ practice) is self-regulated learning without the label or citation. The learning-log advice ("track what you're testing, what you're trying, how it went, what's next") is Zimmerman's self-observation/self-recording cycle.
- `improves → study` (learning-log lesson), and the learning-records culture itself
