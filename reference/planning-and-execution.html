Planning & Execution: How Intentions Become Action

The science of turning "I should do this" into "it is done": why plans fail even when the goal is right, the two measurable errors (underestimating time, overestimating follow-through), and the plan–do–review cycle that closes the gap.

The rest of this shelf tells you how to learn. This doc is about the step before and after: getting the learning scheduled and done. It answers one question — why do good intentions die, and what closes the gap? The answer is threefold. First, the intention–action gap: the brain treats a plan as if it were the action, which is why planning feels like progress and produces none. Second, the planning fallacy: we systematically underestimate time and overestimate follow-through — two measurable, replicated errors, not a character flaw. Third, the fix: a plan–review loop built on task decomposition, if-then triggers, and a weekly reality check. This doc is the planning half of the execute layer; the doing half — cues, friction, scripts — lives in Productivity Systems, and the attention half lives in Focus & Attention.

The intention–action gap — why plans die

Goals and intentions predict behavior far less than people assume. The gap between intending and doing is so robust it has a name — the intention–action gap — and it's the reason advice like "just commit" fails. The classic demonstration (Gollwitzer & Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis of 94 studies) is that intentions alone account for a modest share of behavior change — but that a specific kind of plan closes most of the gap: implementation intentions, the if-then form.

Goal intention vs implementation intention

Goal intention: "I will study for the exam." Names the what — and nothing else. The when, where, and how are left open, which means every moment of execution requires a fresh decision, and a tired brain makes bad decisions at exactly those moments (see Wanting vs Liking: the urge fires at the decision point).

Implementation intention: "When it's 7pm on Tuesday, I will sit at my desk and open the practice-test PDF." Names the situation and the action. The decision is pre-made; execution runs on the cue, not on willpower. Gollwitzer's meta-analysis: implementation intentions produce a medium-to-large effect on follow-through (d ≈ 0.65) — the gap is mostly a specification problem, not a motivation problem.

The productivity doc's scripted actions are this same mechanism applied to daily work; this doc adds the planning layer above it. Two more reasons plans die, both measurable:

  • Planning feels like progress. Making a plan produces a sense of closure (the Zeigarnik effect's flip side: an open loop feels uncomfortable, a planned loop feels closed) — so we stop. Planning is a substitute for doing, and the brain can't always tell the difference. The fix is a rule: a plan is not progress; only execution is. (See Wanting vs Liking — "planning" can be wanting's counterfeit of doing.)
  • Plans assume a stable self. "I'll do it when I feel like it" is a plan that assumes tomorrow's self has today's motivation. Tomorrow's self is tired, distracted, and facing the same urges. Implementation intentions and friction (below) are the fixes for a self that is not stable.

The planning fallacy — two systematic errors

The planning fallacy (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; named and measured by Buehler, Griffin & Ross, 1994) is the replicated finding that people underestimate how long tasks take — even when they know their own history. Two errors stack:

Error What it is Why it happens The fix
Underestimating time We plan as if the task will go perfectly. The "inside view": we imagine our plan's best path and ignore the base rate — how long this kind of task actually took last time (Kahneman & Lovallo, 1993: inside vs outside view). Use the outside view: ask "how long did the last three similar tasks take?" — then add 25–50%. The base rate beats the narrative every time.
Overestimating follow-through We plan as if we'll do everything we schedule. We schedule the ideal day, not the real day — forgetting that energy, attention, and urges are finite and variable. Under-schedule (the Self-Management doc's rule): plan ~60–70% of the honest available time, not 100% of the ideal.

Both errors are corrigible — which is the good news. The planning fallacy is not a fixed trait; it shrinks with explicit base-rate use and with review (below). The mechanism is measurement: if you never record how long things took, your only reference is the fantasy inside your head.

Task decomposition — make the first step tiny

The single most reliable planning technique is breaking a task into steps small enough that the first step is trivial. This works for three distinct reasons:

  • It kills the start problem. A task that fits on one line of a plan ("open the PDF and answer 5 questions") has almost no activation cost. The Zeigarnik effect (the brain's itch to finish what it started) then pulls you into the next step — starting is the hard part, and decomposition makes starting cheap.
  • It converts ambiguity into decisions. "Study biology" is a project; "review the photosynthesis map, then test myself on it" is a step. Ambiguity is a procrastination trigger (see Self-Management — procrastination is emotional, and vagueness is a source of overwhelm).
  • It creates subgoal progress. Small completed steps are visible wins. Visible progress is motivating in the way long-horizon goals are not (the motivation doc's point about proximal rewards — Motivation & Self-Determination).

The rule: if the first step needs willpower, decompose again

A plan is not ready until the first step is something you could do right now, tired, with zero friction — "open the file," "write the first bullet," "put the book on the desk." If the first step still feels heavy, it's not a step; it's a project wearing a step's clothes.

Attach the plan to triggers, not to moods

A plan specifies what. Execution needs when. The evidence-backed way to attach a plan to a moment is the if-then implementation intention; the planning-level version is time-blocking with a trigger:

  • Time-block the plan. Put the step on a specific calendar slot — not a to-do list. A to-do list is a plan without a when; a calendar slot is a plan with a trigger. (Self-management's calendar rule is the same point.)
  • Anchor the block to an existing habit. "After the morning coffee" is a stronger trigger than "sometime tomorrow" — the existing habit is the cue (see Productivity Systems: cues, not motivation, drive execution).
  • Write the if-then for the failure point. The best plans include a contingency: "When I hit a hard problem and want to quit, I will set a 5-minute timer and just re-read the question." This is a plan for the moment the plan would otherwise die.

The plan–do–review loop

Planning is not a one-shot event; it's a loop. The three steps:

Step What you do What it fixes
Plan Decompose the goal, time-block the steps, attach triggers. The intention–action gap (specification).
Do Execute the blocks; note where reality diverged (took longer, felt harder, got interrupted). The planning fallacy (data collection).
Review Once a week: compare plan vs actual. Ask "what took longer than expected? what didn't happen at all? what should I plan next week?" Both errors — the review feeds the outside view back in.

The review is where the planning fallacy actually gets corrected: after a few weeks of plan-vs-actual comparison, your time estimates stop being fantasies because you've replaced the inside view with your own base rate. The productivity doc's weekly ritual is this review built into a system; this doc's contribution is the discipline of recording the actual so the review has data.

One metric is enough: plan vs actual time

You don't need a productivity app. One line per planned block — "planned 60 min, took 90" — is enough to build a personal base rate within weeks. The moment you know your own base rate, you stop scheduling fantasy days, which is most of the fix.

Worked example — planning a two-week exam block

Goal: pass a certification exam in three weeks. The naive plan: "study hard every day." The failure: day one is vague and heavy, so it gets postponed; week one produces guilt, not progress.

The plan-and-review version:

  1. Decompose. List the exam's topics; estimate hours per topic using the outside view (a past exam took X hours per topic → use that, then add 30%).
  2. Time-block. Map topics to calendar slots anchored to existing habits — "after morning coffee: 45 min of retrieval practice on the previous day's topic; evening: 60 min on the new topic." Under-schedule: two blocks a day, not five.
  3. Trigger the failure point. If-then: "When I feel lost on a topic, I will switch to a worked example for 10 minutes rather than quit."
  4. Review. Sunday night: compare the week's plan vs actual. Topics that took 2× the estimate get re-estimated (the fallacy shrinks). Topics that didn't happen at all get cut or re-sequenced — not added to a longer list.

The difference from the naive plan: every day has a specific, tiny-first-step, trigger-anchored action; and the plan is self-correcting, because the review feeds reality back into next week's plan.

Most common failure modes

Failure Why it fails The fix
Planning instead of doing The plan produces closure, so the loop feels complete. Rule: a plan is not progress. Count execution, not planning.
Fantasy scheduling (100% ideal days) Overestimating follow-through; one missed block collapses the plan. Under-schedule to ~60–70% of honest time; expect misses.
No base rate Every plan is a fresh inside-view fantasy. Record plan vs actual; use your own history.
Vague first steps "Study biology" has no trigger and high activation cost. Decompose until the first step is trivial.
No review The loop never closes; errors repeat weekly. Weekly 15-minute plan-vs-actual review (the productivity doc's ritual).
Plans without triggers A to-do list has no when; execution requires a fresh decision. Time-block; anchor to existing habits; write the if-then.
Plans that never change Reality diverges; a rigid plan becomes a source of guilt, not a guide. Plans are hypotheses; the review updates them.

Principles — the shared constants

  1. A plan is a specification, not a promise. Specify when, where, and the first tiny step.
  2. The base rate beats the narrative. Use your own history, then add margin.
  3. Decompose until the first step is trivial. Starting is the hard part; make it cheap.
  4. Attach plans to triggers, not moods. Calendar slots and if-then scripts, not "when I feel like it."
  5. Under-schedule honestly. 60–70% of real time beats 100% of fantasy time.
  6. Review weekly, adjust always. The loop, not the plan, is the system.
  7. Plan for the failure point. Write the if-then for the moment the plan usually dies.
  8. Planning is not progress. Execution is the only thing that counts.

Summary — the mental checklist

  • The gap: intentions → action is bridged by specification, not motivation. If-then implementation intentions close most of it (Gollwitzer meta: d ≈ 0.65).
  • The fallacy: we underestimate time and overestimate follow-through. Fix with the outside view (base rate + margin) and under-scheduling.
  • The loop: plan (decompose + time-block + trigger) → do (record actual) → review (weekly plan-vs-actual).
  • The rule: planning feels like progress; it isn't. Execution counts.
