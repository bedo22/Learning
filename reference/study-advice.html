Reframe how you think about studying

Studying ≠ learning

Studying is the activity (writing notes, reading, testing). Learning is the outcome — it's only happened if you can remember, understand deeply, and apply. Methods sit on a spectrum of effectiveness: rereading and highlighting produce almost no real learning. Ask of each method: how much learning is this producing?

Stop looking for study hacks

Real ones don't exist. If a 30-second hack could fix your problem, you'd have fixed it already. The big blockers are years of stacked habits — untrainable overnight. Chasing hacks wastes time and, worse, convinces you you're just "not smart enough" when they fail.

Build deep processing — don't copy successful students

Copying what top students do doesn't work; millions do the same things and don't get the same results. The difference is deep processing ability — and it's trainable, not genetic. Justin Sung built his deliberately (he wasn't naturally "an anomaly"). This is the same muscle as higher-order thinking.

Stop thinking techniques, start thinking systems

There is no perfect technique. Think of techniques as tools in a toolbox — each has a purpose and limitations. You can't build a house with one screwdriver. Don't abandon a tool because it has limits; use it for what it's good for and supplement. The classic abuse: flashcards for everything — "not working? more flashcards." Everyone already has a learning system; it's just unrefined. See the learning system.

Avoid the illusion of learning

The learning equivalent of productive procrastination: doing something that feels like learning but isn't. The classic: 3 hours writing notes after a 1-hour lecture, then admitting "if tested now, I'd do terribly." It's comfortable habit, not learning. Test: "if I were tested right now, how would I do?" If the answer is "terribly," you studied, you didn't learn. Related to the myth that learning should feel easy.

Test yourself the right way

Test yourself earlier and more often

Don't save practice tests for the week before the exam — by then you find gaps with no time to fill them. Weekly + monthly cadence: every Saturday, test yourself on Mon–Fri; last Saturday of the month, test the whole month. Gaps are small and quick to fill, and by exam day you know how you'll do. If you walk in not knowing — or feeling confident but scoring differently — you're not testing early enough, often enough, or in the right way.

Test at each level of knowledge

Don't only test at a low level (flashcards, single facts) when you'll be examined at a higher level (interconnected, complex application). If you never test whether you can connect concepts, you'll struggle on exactly the complicated questions. Ask: how am I going to be tested on this? how do I need to use my knowledge? — then test yourself that same way.

Test yourself in different ways (declarative vs procedural)

Two types of knowledge: declarative (the what — concepts, definitions, understanding) and procedural (the how — executing, solving, doing). Most subjects need both. Math over-focuses on procedural (solving equations) and fails on conceptual questions; coding needs both (understanding the language vs writing bug-free code). Test both, in the balance your subject demands.

Assume you'll make every mistake again (no "silly mistakes")

Silly mistake syndrome: blaming losses on "silly mistakes" when they're really knowledge gaps. If you couldn't generate the answer — even if the answer sheet makes sense when you read it — there's a gap. The people who consistently make "silly mistakes" are almost always the same people with knowledge gaps. Assume every mistake is a real gap and dig until you find it.

Use memorization deliberately, not as a default

Do spaced repetition & active recall properly

The spacing part is easy and much less important than how you do active recall. The benefit comes from free recall, not cued recall, and definitely not recognition. Two practical fixes: (a) swap your cues regularly — if you can answer a flashcard from the first 3 words, you're testing word-matching, not recall; (b) never let recognition masquerade as recall — clicking "show answer," thinking "oh yeah I knew that" is the ultimate illusion (you just proved you didn't know it). Worry more about active-recall quality than spacing perfection.

A concrete spacing schedule — the 2/7/30 rhythm

Spacing quality matters less than recall quality, but a concrete rhythm removes the "when do I review?" decision. The classic pattern, fitted to a course calendar: review after 2 days, again after 7, again after 30 (roughly: day 2, day 9, day 39 from first encounter). Day-2 catches the steepest part of the forgetting curve, day-7 moves it into longer-term storage, day-30 lands it before the exam. Adjust the gaps to your exam date (compress for a 2-week exam, stretch for a semester) — the principle is increasing gaps, not the exact numbers. Same idea appears in the forgetting-curve doc and the play doc's rest menu.

What the evidence says — the effectiveness ranking

The 2013 ten-techniques mega-review (Dunlosky et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest) ranks practice testing and distributed practice as the two most effective techniques; rereading, highlighting, and summarization score low. The testing effect is among the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology — from its modern origin (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006: tests beat restudying even with no feedback) through lab meta-analysis (Rowland, 2014), classroom meta-analysis (Adesope et al., 2017, 217 studies), and applied systematic review (Agarwal et al., 2021), all agree: testing beats restudying, and the benefit survives the real classroom. That's the research backing for the testing and spacing lessons above.

Minimize rote memorization

Rote memorization scales badly: every flashcard creates recurring debt (you must review it forever or lose it). A small reduction in how much you rote-memorize yields a big time saving — one student cut rote from 90%→70% and his study week dropped from 60 to 30 hours. Treat it as a last resort; if you can learn it properly the first time (with higher-order encoding), do that instead of multiplying future work. Related: flashcard overwhelm.

For rote memorization, use memory palaces & link methods

When rote memorization is the right tool (last resort), use proven techniques: memory palaces (loci method) and link methods. They're very effective for their purpose — just don't abuse them beyond it. Flashcards work too, for the right content.

Learn with others & ahead of the curve

Study with friends — to find each other's gaps

Two benefits. (a) Accountability & focus — but only with shared purpose, structure, assigned roles, and timers (see accountability). (b) Make practice tests for each other (the underrated one): write an exam from memory, check it against notes, make a model answer sheet, then swap with friends. No answer sheet means you must critically verify your answers — and where your answers differ from your friend's, you've found deep, nuanced gaps for discussion. With 3–4 friends, one exam's effort yields 3 extra exams.

Study ahead to get ahead (priming / scoping)

If you're falling behind, don't try to catch up first — plug the leak first (sinking ship: you don't bail water before plugging the hole). The fastest way: scope every subject before you learn it — get a big-picture overview (main ideas, how they connect) without memorizing details. This is called priming, and it has a very high correlation with learning. It gives your brain context and relevance — the "why" and "where" before the "what." Library analogy: spend 15 minutes learning the shelving layout before books start arriving, and each book finds its place; without that, every book is a mystery pile.

Make and critique analogies

Actively forming analogies is criminally underrated — it forces comparison and high cognitive load. But go further: critique your analogies. "Life is like a journey" → but life is less predictable → "life is like a spontaneous journey" → through a foggy forest → … Each critique forces deeper, more critical examination of the topic and induces higher cognitive load → deeper learning. Test it: analogy-alone vs analogy-critiqued — the critiqued one wins 99/100.

Keep a learning log

Track: what am I testing? what do I think will make a difference? what technique am I trying today? how did it go? what's next? Justin Sung wishes he'd kept one — he estimates it would have turned 3 years of improvement into 9 months. You can only change 1–2 things at a time (a biological limit), so over a long journey you'll lose track without a log. It's also motivating: looking back shows progress you can't see day-to-day. (This is self-regulated learning's self-observation/self-recording cycle — Zimmerman, 2002 — and this workspace's learning-records/ folder is exactly this.)

Test day — the exam strategy

All the testing above is preparation; the exam itself has its own small science of performance. The principles here are the same ones — retrieval, calibration, and not trusting fluency — applied in the room:

  • Read the whole paper before writing. Sixty seconds of scoping ("which questions, what marks, where's the easy win") beats starting on question 1. This is the same priming you use when learning a subject — the big picture before the details.
  • Budget by marks, not by order. Allocate time proportionally to marks; never get stuck on a hard question while easy marks sit unclaimed. Parkinson's law applies to exams: time expands to fill the question.
  • Attempt everything from memory first. Your first instinct is your retrieval; don't overwrite it with doubt. If a question stumps you, mark it and return — the spacing effect works even inside an exam: later questions often prime the answer you couldn't retrieve.
  • Don't "re-learn" during the exam. Reading the question sheet hoping to learn the topic is the fluency illusion under time pressure. If you don't know it, you don't know it — allocate your remaining time to questions where retrieval will pay.
  • Predict your score before the paper comes back. Calibrate yourself against the result — this is the learning log habit applied to exam performance itself.

AI and learning — the 2024–2026 evidence

Everything above was written before generative AI arrived — but the evidence since ChatGPT says the principles hold, and the stakes went up. Two RCTs bracket the problem: AI that answers for you harms real learning (higher practice scores, worse exams — Bastani et al., NBER 2024; PNAS 2025) while AI that interrogates you roughly doubles it (Kestin et al., Scientific Reports, 2025). Same technology, opposite design: the difference is who does the retrieving.

The rule: keep the generation on your side

This is the page's opening lesson applied to the newest tool — studying ≠ learning; generating is learning. Use AI as an interrogator, not an answer machine: attempt first, ask it to quiz and probe rather than produce, and beware the fluency illusion — a great AI answer makes material feel easy, which is the opposite of learning. Full treatment — operating rules, what AI is genuinely good for, and the calibration check — in AI & Learning (Ref 15).

The throughline: Most of these 16 are applications of three principles already in your references — encoding & retrieval, higher-order thinking, and working-memory handling. The new layer here is the practice: when to test, how to test, what to minimize, and how to iterate with a log.

Worked example — one course, one week, the system applied

Anatomy course, 8 lecture topics, exam in 3 weeks. Week 1: after each lecture, one brain-dump page from memory (retrieval, not rereading), then fill gaps in a different color. Weekend: build one page of higher-order questions per topic (why does this structure matter — level 5–6, not list-the-parts). Weeks 2–3: the 2/7/30 rhythm — today's material retested after 2 days, last week's after 7, the first week's after 30. Wrong answers go on a separate "error log" page (Metcalfe: errors are the curriculum) and get retested at the next session.

The audit each Sunday: what did I produce from memory? (pages written, not pages read) · which questions did I generate? · what's still wrong twice? (those become the week's priority) · did I study with a friend's gaps on the table?

Summary — the mental checklist

  • The reframe: studying ≠ learning; techniques don't matter, systems do.
  • The engine: retrieval first, spaced (2/7/30), interleaved once basics exist, at every knowledge level.
  • The fuel: desirable difficulty — if it feels smooth, it's probably not encoding.
  • The instruments: brain-dump pages, generated questions, error logs, the learning log.
  • The AI rule: keep generation on your side (the AI doc's bracket applies to every technique here).
