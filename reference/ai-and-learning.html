AI & Learning: The 2024–2026 Evidence and How to Use It

Same technology, opposite design — the two RCTs that bracket the problem

العربية

Generative AI arrived after most of this shelf was written — but the evidence since ChatGPT says the principles hold, and the stakes went up. The headline finding is not "AI helps" or "AI hurts." It is that the same technology doubles learning or destroys it depending on one design decision: who does the retrieving. This doc is the AI-era update to the whole shelf.

The two RCTs that bracket the problem

  • AI that answers for you harms learning. In a randomized trial in high-school math (Bastani et al., NBER 2024; PNAS 2025), students using an unconstrained GPT scored higher on practice problems but worse on the real exam than the control group — and were unaware of it. The tool made practice feel like learning while replacing the retrieval that builds memory.
  • AI that interrogates you helps. In a large Harvard physics-course RCT (Kestin et al., Scientific Reports, 2025), a Socratic AI tutor that prompts students to retrieve and explain — without giving answers away — produced roughly double the learning gains in less time than in-class active learning.
  • Offloading thinking to AI correlates with weaker thinking. Heavier AI-tool use tracked with more cognitive offloading and lower critical-thinking scores, most strongly in younger users (Gerlich, 2025 — correlational; treat as a warning sign, not proof).

The rule in one line: keep the generation on your side

The lesson is exactly the one the shelf opened with — studying ≠ learning; generating is learning. This is the play doc's wanting-vs-liking split applied to studying: the tool can feel productive (wanting — fluency, effortlessness) while delivering no learning (liking — retention, recall). If you can't reproduce the answer without the tool, it wasn't yours.

Why unguarded AI harms: the fluency illusion on steroids

Everything in Metacognition & Calibration predicts the Bastani result. Your monitor reads fluency, not memory: a perfect AI answer makes the material feel effortless, your judgment of learning spikes, and you stop — with nothing actually encoded. AI doesn't just fail to teach; it actively corrupts your calibration, because the "I got it" feeling is now stronger than ever and even further from the truth. The danger is precisely that it feels so good.

Worse, the practice-problem result shows the deception is structural: the tool inflates in-session performance (the AI does the work) while the delayed measure (the exam) collapses. That gap is the wanting/liking gap made measurable.

How to use AI well — the operating rules

  • Attempt first. Write your answer or explanation before opening the tool, then use it to find gaps — not to fill the page for you.
  • Ask it to quiz and probe, not to produce. "Ask me questions on X and tell me where my reasoning breaks" beats "summarize X." The Kestin tutor worked because it did exactly this.
  • Make it an interrogator, not an answer machine. Use it the way you'd use the Feynman method: it should push you to retrieve, then critique your retrieval.
  • Beware the fluency illusion. A great AI answer makes the material feel easy — and feeling easy is the opposite of learning. If you didn't generate it, it's not yours yet.
  • Verify by reproduction, not recognition. Close the chat and reproduce the answer cold. That's the only test that counts — and it's the same recall-not-recognition discipline as ever.

What AI is genuinely good for

Used inside the retrieval loop — never outside it — AI is an excellent infrastructure tool:

  • Generating practice questions at the level you'll be examined (see Bloom's six levels): "make me application-level questions on this topic, then grade my answers against the rubric."
  • Diagnosing your attempts. Paste your answer and ask where your reasoning breaks — the critique targets your generation, so the learning stays yours.
  • Spaced-repetition logistics. Drafting flashcards, scheduling reviews, building interleaved problem sets — the system work, not the thinking.
  • Explaining after retrieval fails. The correct order: attempt → check → if stuck, ask for the next step, not the answer → re-attempt.

The line to hold: AI may do everything except the retrieval. The moment it generates the thing you were supposed to generate, it has crossed from tool to crutch.

The calibration warning — and the check

The diagnostic is identical to the liking test

Just as play must be judged by liking during, not wanting before, AI-assisted study must be judged by cold reproduction after, not fluency during. Test yourself without the tool, on a delay, at the level you'll actually be examined. If you pass that, the tool was a trainer. If you don't, it was a crutch — regardless of how good the sessions felt.
