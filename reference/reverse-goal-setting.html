Reverse Goal Setting: Become the Person, Not Just the Outcome

From Dr. Justin Sung · "How I Changed My Life in 1 Year with Reverse Goal Setting"

Justin Sung left medicine in May 2019; one year later he was the most fulfilled he'd ever been. The method: reverse goal setting — start with the goal and work backwards to a clear path of action. He now uses it for everything important and complicated, and never uses normal goal setting anymore.

The problem with normal goal setting

Normal goal setting focuses on the what but not the how. You set "get into medical school," then ask "what can I do to get closer?" — study more, study harder, be diligent. For simple goals this works (he got in, but admits he got lucky). For anything complicated, the path is unclear, and you waste time and energy figuring it out — or burn out and never arrive. The uncertainty is what makes it hard.

Don't set goals too far into the future

Justin Sung's 15-year-old self set "become a doctor" with attached expectations: financial stability, fulfillment, enjoyable work. Ten years later, having achieved it, much of that didn't materialize as expected. Looking 5–10 years ahead, there's too much uncertainty — about yourself, your situation, and the goal itself. Stay flexible and adaptable; don't lock into a pathway just because you declared it. Plan in shorter sprints and re-evaluate constantly.

Step 1 · Define the goal

  1. Write your long-term vision/direction (5–10 years) — keep it broad.
  2. Under it, write why — the feeling or situation you think it'll give you. Mentally give yourself permission to pivot if the goal stops serving the why.
  3. Break it to a medium-term goal (≤3 years). Beyond 3 years, plans get fragile — one broken link derails everything.

Sub-step: specify the requirements (grade boundaries, exams, etc.). If you hit "I don't know what the requirements are," that's not a dead end — it's your immediate next step: go learn what they are.

Step 2 · Define your future self (the meta-goal)

Outcomes are symptoms of the process

A genie offers one wish. "I wish to win the lottery and have a million dollars" is a bad wish — most lottery winners lose it all, because they have the outcome but not the skills, habits, and attributes to keep or grow wealth. A better wish: "give me the skills, attributes, and habits that let me make a million dollars."

The meta-goal is not the outcome — it's the person for whom achieving the goal would be easy. Outcomes can't be controlled; they're symptoms of the process you can control. A person who can't engage the right process (or doesn't even know what it is) is unlikely to reach the outcome.

Ask: who is the person that would find this goal easy, and what skills, attributes, habits, and opportunities do they have? Be detailed and specific. Justin Sung recommends five core areas to start with — each cross-linked to its reference:

  1. Time management — Self-Management
  2. Task management & prioritization — Eisenhower matrix
  3. Focus & concentration — focus / recovery
  4. Procrastination — procrastination (emotional)
  5. Learning ability — The Learning System + Orders of Learning

This meta-goal — becoming that person — becomes your primary #1 goal.

Step 3 · Define your current self

For each skill/attribute/habit you identified, rate the requirement out of 10, then rate your current ability out of 10. The gap between them becomes your plan.

"I don't know" is a gap to fix, not a dead end

If you don't know what skills the goal requires — that becomes step one: talk to people, read, learn what the requirements are (of the person, not just the goal). If you don't know your current ability — you lack self-awareness, and most people do until they direct attention at it. The most underrated activity to begin with: observe and reflect — be objective and honest about what you actually do and who you are.

Step 4 · Force field analysis

Examine the forces pushing you toward or holding you back from the goal: what barriers must you overcome, and what resources/opportunities do you already have access to? Justin Sung realized his prioritization was weak and he lacked entrepreneurship experience — but he had a strong network and resources. Revisit this every few months whenever you're unsure how to keep progressing.

Step 5 · Build your plan (what, when, how)

You know the goal, the meta-goal (who to become), where you stand, and the forces at play. Now decide what to focus on first, when, and how.

The bottleneck principle — the what may not be what you expect

A student struggling at university had a wide learning-skills gap, so they tried to train it — but she wasn't consistent. Digging deeper: she was being abused at home. You can't work on learning skills without a foundation of stability and safety. The #1 priority shifted to getting her a job so she could move out. A month later she moved; then the learning-skills work took off. Less extreme but common: people think they need better learning skills, but the real blocker is procrastination or an unwillingness to try new things. Step back and evaluate holistically — the bottleneck is often upstream of where you think.

One or two things at a time; be conservative with timelines

Changing skills and habits takes longer than you expect, and the more you try to improve at once, the harder each one gets. Justin Sung focused on only 1–2 things per month, deliberately stopping himself from the other 10. Be conservative with timelines — especially for habits you've had for years. (This mirrors the learning-log principle: you can only change 1–2 things at a time.) The plan you build enters your calendar — daily, evenings, weekends. As you grow as a person, the outcomes you can naturally achieve grow with it.

How it fits: Reverse goal setting is the "why and who" that sits above Self-Management and Productivity Systems (the "how"). It tells you which skills to develop; those skill areas are the rest of this course. Outcomes are symptoms of the process — so become the person for whom the outcome is a natural consequence.
