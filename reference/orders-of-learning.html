Orders of Learning: Higher vs Lower Order

From Dr. Justin Sung · "Learn to Learn" — Part 3

Justin Sung's orders of learning are his practical two-level model of how integrated your knowledge is. It gives you a goalpost: how deep to go, which retrieval strategy to pick, and how to encode upfront. (It pairs with — but simplifies — Bloom's six levels: higher-order ≈ Bloom's upper levels.)

OrderWhat it isMemory & decay
Lower orderIsolated facts & concepts — recite a definition, regurgitate a fact, explain one thing in isolation. Early schooling lives here.Fast decay — not in a network, so the brain drops it. Fine for a small set of must-memorize facts (sometimes more efficient to just flashcard them).
Higher orderIntegrated — a concept matters because of its influence on other things; you see how components connect and interact ("if I change this, that goes down").Forces info into a schema / network → stronger memory → the flatter decay curve from The Learning System.

The goalpost

Knowing the order tells you: how deep to learn something, which retrieval method matches that depth, and how to encode when the information first arrives. When new info enters, "put on your higher-order hat": how does this connect? what's the pattern? where does it fit?

Strategies — but the principle matters more than the tactic

Any method works if it forces thinking in networks and relationships. Specific tactics Justin Sung recommends to start:

Mind mapping encoding
Effective only when done correctly — you must genuinely evaluate, prioritize, and restructure to find the best connections. Just drawing boxes and arrows without thinking deeply is "mind mapping without higher-order thinking" — ineffective.
Creating analogies encoding
Take related concepts, find how they connect, and re-represent them as something else; then test "does this analogy still make sense?" That test forces you to examine whether you understood each piece correctly. Use frequently.
Feynman method (teach a 10-year-old) retrieval
Explain from memory in the simplest terms — you prioritize, restructure, and check accuracy. It's higher-order (networks), generative (you produce it), and free recall (from memory) at once. Teach an imaginary 10-year-old.

Principle > tactic. You don't have to use mind maps, analogies, or the Feynman method. Any technique works as long as it makes you think in relationships — which is more important relative to something else, and how it all connects — instead of memorizing isolated facts.

The hard part isn't the thinking — it's breaking the habit

Most people can think in relationships. The real challenge is that we have strong lower-order habits: it feels wrong, destabilizing, and uncertain to not try to remember and understand each isolated thing. The skill is being okay with: "I don't fully understand it yet, I definitely won't remember it — but I understand enough to start seeing how it fits the big picture." Then connect, group, arrange.
