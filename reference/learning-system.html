The Learning System: Encoding & Retrieval

From Dr. Justin Sung · "Learn to Learn" — Part 2 (with the science of memory)

العربية

Don't think of learning as a pile of techniques used now and then. See it as a system with two components. When something's off (too slow, poor memory, can't apply), you find which component is weak and fix that.

The two components of the learning system
ComponentWhat it isIf it's poor
EncodingTurning new information into memory — finding patterns, angles, connections so the brain sees it as relevant and worth keeping.Poor retention, superficial understanding, can't apply to complex problems.
RetrievalPulling stored memory out and using it (explain, solve, answer). Also re-encodes — a cycle.Low fluency, undetected knowledge gaps, can't use knowledge the way you need it.

The forgetting curve & why encoding is the real prize

Memory fades — knowledge decay along the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve. Spaced retrieval (test yourself after meaningful forgetting, then re-encode) flattens the curve each time — Justin Sung notes "spaced repetition" is not technically the right term; retrieval is the active part.

But retrieval isn't the only lever. Better encoding flattens the curve from the start.
Poor encoding → steep decay → you must retrieve every 2–3 days or lose it → flashcard overwhelm (pushing a boulder uphill; miss a day and it rolls back).
Good encoding → shallow decay → retrieve every 2–3 weeks and it holds. You've made the hill less steep. Encoding is the most life-changing skill to learn.

Curiosity is a memory amplifier

In a landmark brain-imaging study, states of high curiosity boosted hippocampus-dependent learning — and the boost spilled over to incidental material learned while curious, via the dopaminergic circuit (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, Neuron, 2014). Practical upshot for encoding: chase the question before the answer. Prime a topic with a "why" you genuinely want answered — the encoding that follows is stickier. (This is the neuroscience behind priming.)

Which to learn first? Retrieval — counterintuitively

Encoding is the bigger prize, but it takes months to years to rewire how your brain processes information. Retrieval skills are quick to learn and pay off immediately. If you over-invest in encoding before a retrieval baseline, your performance drops during the long gap before encoding clicks. Path: raise retrieval first; let encoding climb slowly alongside.

Doing retrieval right: method × frequency

Frequency — start with a 1-day → 1-week → 1-month template, then assess retention: if it's dropping too fast, move reviews closer; if still strong, spread them out. Each top-up slows decay for that item.

Method — match the method to how you'll actually use the knowledge (your "northern star"). This is encoding specificity: what you encoded determines what you can later retrieve and with what prompts (Tulving & Thomson, 1973). Spectrum from weak to strong:

MethodExampleStrength
RecognitionFlashcard flip, multiple choiceWeakest — can hit 90% retention yet only ~50% functional ability.
Cued recallFill-in-the-blank (the sentence gives context)Medium.
Free recall (generative)"Here's a problem — solve it from everything you've learned." No cues.Strongest — you generate and manipulate the knowledge.

Push for generative (do something with it — make a question, solve, explain, write) and manipulative (combine facts: "now that I know X, what does that mean for Y?"). Struggling here isn't failure — it finds the gaps, which is a core purpose of retrieval.

For working professionals: opportunistic > deliberate

Deliberate retrieval = a separate scheduled session (flashcards, quizzes) — extra work on your plate. Opportunistic retrieval = fold retrieval into work you already do. Learn something for a project, then teach it from memory in the next day's team briefing — you consolidate your own knowledge, find gaps, and create value at once. Save deliberate sessions for knowledge you don't use daily.
