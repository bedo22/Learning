Self-Management: Time, Tasks & Focus

From Dr. Justin Sung · "Learn to Learn" — Part 4

Great learning skills with useless self-management = useless. Self-management is what gives you the time, energy, and concentration to do the learning. Three parts: time, tasks, focus.

1 · Time management — schedule, then face reality

  • Maintain a calendar with blocks for everything — work, learning, family, chores, sleep, exercise, coffee with a friend. This alone solves most "time management" problems.
  • Track your time to see how long things actually take vs. how you planned. Your schedules fail because they're fantasy, not reality.
  • Under-schedule > over-schedule. Plan to do less than you'd like. Over-scheduling = always behind, always guilty. Under-scheduling is honest and plannable.

Feeling "I never have enough time" is rarely a time problem. Once you schedule realistically, the leftover pain is a task-management problem — you have too much on the list. Prioritization is the fix.

2 · Task management — the Eisenhower matrix

Sort tasks by urgent vs important. The trap: the brain feels urgency, not long-term importance. Measure importance by the consequence of not doing it.

ImportantNot important
UrgentFocus / Do nowDelegate / minimize — assess the real consequence; you can often handle it.
Not urgentSchedule — the life-changing quadrant (health, skills). By the time it's urgent, it's too late.Delete — raise your importance threshold; put as much here as possible. Better to rest than do these.

Two traps: (1) the urgency trap — doing only urgent things, never the important-but-not-urgent ones; (2) not deleting enough — clinging to low-value tasks out of fear of trivial consequences. Both keep you busy but stalled.

3 · Focus & attention — short-term vs long-term

Short-term fixes (app blockers, etc.) are easy and effective but not sustainable — the effect fades or you find new distractions. Long-term fixes address the root cause but take months. Use short-term fixes freely, but know they're bridges, not cures.

Procrastination is emotional, not distraction

Procrastination is a coping mechanism for the discomfort/overwhelm of anticipated effort — you pick an easier path to dodge the feeling. Block Instagram and you'll browse the app store for games. The distractions aren't the problem; the avoidance is.

Short-term: app blockers; make the productive path and the distraction path both high-effort (effort vs effort is an easier choice); accountability — a partner/group works because it swaps fragile dopamine-based motivation for durable identity-based motivation ("I'm someone who keeps my word"). Reward/consequence apps can feed the dopamine problem.

Long-term (practice for months):

Get good at being bored
Sit and do nothing; feel the restlessness and stay. That restlessness is what derails your focus daily — practicing sitting through it trains the return-to-focus muscle.
Get good at doing hard things
Train the brain that effort ≠ bad. Lean on the Zeigarnik effect: the brain likes to finish what's started. When resistance hits, don't aim to finish — just start (open tabs, gather resources, scaffold). Starting makes continuing far easier; half the time you keep going.
Mindfulness
Mindful dishwashing / walking / breathing — attend fully, and each time the mind wanders, bring it back. Boring breathing is best practice: lots of wandering = lots of reps of returning. Being okay with boredom is the training; it transfers to workday focus.

Putting it together: basic task prioritization + a minimum of time management + a learning system of decent encoding & retrieval at the higher orders = better memory, deeper understanding, complex problem-solving → better results, for exams and work.
