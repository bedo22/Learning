Learning Myths That Block Improvement

From Dr. Justin Sung · "Learn to Learn" — Part 1

Justin Sung opens with myths because they are fundamental blockers: if you believe them, tactics won't help. Over 95% of people hold at least one. Each myth below has a correction that reframes the problem.

Why these myths persist: they all share one mechanism — they feel true. Learning styles feel true because we have preferences; easy-learning feels true because rereading feels fluent; multitasking feels true because switching is fast. Feeling fluent is not evidence of learning — that gap is the fluency illusion, and every myth below is a fluency illusion wearing a costume. (The one quantitative claim above — "20–30% more effective" — is Sung's framing of efficiency gains, not a measured effect size; treat it as motivation, not measurement.)

Myth 1 — "I don't have enough time" / "I'm not smart enough"

The time myth and the IQ myth: I'm too slow because I lack time, IQ, or memory. Partially true — but the fix isn't more hours or better genes. Being 20–30% more effective (deeper understanding, better connection-making, 20–30% more attention) frees 20–30% of your time — an evening, a weekend, headroom for harder problems (a promotion, a raise). Everyone can gain 20–30%; most people are far from their ceiling because they've never been taught how to learn.

Myth 2 — "I have a learning style"

The learning styles theory says each person has one best modality (visual / auditory / read-write / kinesthetic) and should double down on it. The research is conclusive — so conclusive that the field has moved on to studying why people still believe it. It feels true because we do have learning preferences, but those are different:

Myth 3 — "Learning should feel easy"

When a strategy makes you work harder, get confused, and think deeper, you read that discomfort as "this isn't working" and switch back to an easier-feeling method. Justin Sung calls this a counterproductive adjustment — you abandon the effective strategy for the ineffective one because it feels easier.

The discomfort is the mechanism, not a warning. Effective, efficient learning costs mental effort — not more hours, but deeper thinking in the hours you spend. This is the misinterpreted effort hypothesis: the effort of higher-order thinking is what builds retention. See the Bloom reference for the full principle.

What you need isn't easy learning — it's effective and efficient learning. The cost is effort.

More myths the research has settled

Myth 4 — "Brain-training games make me smarter"

Computerized "brain training" (Lumosity-style) produces real gains only on the trained task and things very like it; there is no reliable transfer to general intelligence, working memory, or everyday reasoning. The definitive verdict — a multi-lab review by the field's own leaders — is that the marketing outran the evidence (Simons et al., 2016). If your goal is durable learning, spend the time on retrieval practice and spacing instead: those effects are large, replicated, and transfer.

Myth 5 — "A growth mindset is the key to achievement"

Mindset matters, but the pop version is oversold. Two meta-analyses found the mindset–achievement relationship is weak and intervention effects are small (Sisk et al., 2018) — and the famous national experiment that did find an effect showed it was conditional: it helped lower-achieving students in a supportive school context, not everyone (Yeager et al., Nature, 2019). The honest takeaway is the same as Myth 1: beliefs matter less than effortful, well-designed process. Your time is better spent on technique than on affirmations.

Myth 6 — "Multitasking is efficient"

The brain doesn't multitask — it switches, and every switch costs time and accuracy (task-switching costs are among the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology; Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans, 2001). Studying while messaging feels productive because the switching is fast; the measured cost is slower, shallower processing of both streams. Worse, the presence of a phone — even face-down, even off — measurably drains attention (Ward et al., 2017), and laptop multitasking in a lecture harms not just the multitasker but the students who can see their screen (Sana et al., 2013). The full evidence is in Productivity Systems and Self-Management.

Myth 7 — "We only use 10% of our brain"

False in every sense the pop version intends: brain imaging shows activity across the whole brain even during rest, no silent 90% exists, and damage to almost any region produces measurable deficits. The myth persists because it feels motivational ("imagine the untapped potential") — but the real untapped potential is process, not brain tissue: the 20–30% effectiveness gains of Myth 1 come from better encoding and retrieval, not from "unlocking" dormant neurons.

Myth 8 — "I'm a left-brain / right-brain learner"

The hemispheres differ in function, but the pop version — "logical left-brain people vs creative right-brain people, learn to your side" — is a neuromyth with no supporting evidence and a strong dose of the learning-styles problem (it's really just learning styles wearing a brain scan). The honest science: complex tasks recruit both hemispheres, and the dichotomy predicts nothing about how you should study. See Myth 2 for what actually helps: mixed-modal, well-encoded learning.
