Bloom's Six Levels of Thinking

From Dr. Justin Sung · adapted from Bloom's revised taxonomy (1956, rev. 2001)

Every piece of studying happens at a level of thinking. Justin Sung splits each level into two halves: the thinking you do, and the result it unlocks. Higher levels cost more mental effort, but build deeper knowledge and stronger memory.

The six levels — read top-down, but study by starting at level 5

Each level has two halves: the thinking you do and the result it unlocks. Higher levels cost more mental effort, but build deeper knowledge and stronger memory.

The six levels — see "start at the top" below for how to use them.
#Thinking → ResultWhat it looks like
L1Remember → RegurgitateRote: rereading, rewriting, mass flashcards. Output is listing, defining, stating facts. The high-school default — and a weak way to retain.
L2Understand → ExplainComprehend what it means. Same technique as L1, different intent. Output: explain-a-concept questions. The bulk of early assessments.
L3Apply → SolveUse one learned concept to solve a "one-to-one" problem — plug into a formula, or build a simple function. Practice papers and quizzes live here.
L4Analyze → CompareCompare & contrast — see information in relation to other information. Venn diagrams, comparison tables, mind maps. The first higher-order level; it feels harder.
L5Evaluate → Prioritize"So what? Why does it matter?" Form a conclusion and justify it. The top-learner level; you jump between sources to answer "why does this matter?"
L6Create → GenerateDesign or produce something new. Advanced; most people's daily peak is level 5.

Start at the top and work down

The counterintuitive move: start at the top and work down.
Most people try to climb 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5. That's too slow, and you forget as you go — knowledge decay along the forgetting curve means you spend most of your time relearning.

Instead, start at level 5 (Evaluate) and work down. The brain builds memory more strongly at higher levels, so the lower-level results — regurgitate, explain, solve — arrive as side effects. It does not work in reverse: studying only to regurgitate will never auto-produce comparison or judgment.

Practical rule: when you sit down to study, don't aim to remember or understand. Aim to evaluate — which forces you to analyze and understand in the first place.

The effort is the mechanism, not a warning

Jumping from L3 to L4 makes thinking noticeably harder and slower. Many learners read that as "I'm doing it wrong" and drop back to easy levels — so they never improve. Justin Sung calls this the misinterpreted effort hypothesis. The effort isn't a bug; it's what builds deeper knowledge and stronger memory.

The research agrees: Bjork's desirable difficulties — conditions that make learning harder at first (spacing, retrieval, interleaving, higher-order testing) — consistently produce better long-term retention and transfer, and learners reliably misjudge them as less effective (Bjork & Bjork, 2011; Rowland, 2014). The discomfort is a feature, not a bug: it's the signature of storage strength being built.

How the levels map to the orders of learning

The orders of learning divide practice into lower order (isolated facts — fast decay) and higher order (integrated networks — durable memory). The mapping to Bloom's levels is direct: L1–L3 are lower-order work — each question touches one concept in isolation, so nothing is forced into a network; L4–L6 are higher-order work — comparing, prioritizing, and generating all force relations between concepts, which is exactly what builds schemas. This is also why "start at level 5" and "practice higher-order" are the same advice arriving from two directions.

A study tip: predict the exam's questions

Bloom's taxonomy was built for educators and exam writers, and almost every curriculum is designed around it. So ask an AI (ChatGPT / Gemini):

Give me questions at [your stage, e.g. 2nd-year uni] for [subject, e.g. microbiology] at Bloom's revised taxonomy level [4]

This lets you predict — and practise — the exact question types an examiner uses to separate good from great.

Use Bloom's to interrogate AI answers too. An AI answer is a piece of content — evaluate it the way you'd evaluate your own study: does it just state (L1–L2) or does it compare, judge, and generate (L4–L6)? Ask it to analyze and evaluate ("what are the weaknesses of this argument? which of these two claims is better supported and why?") — then do that thinking yourself. The level of the question you ask sets the level of the learning you do. (See Study Advice · AI and learning.)
