# Source digest — Locke & Latham, 2002

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Specific, difficult goals improve performance** — goals that are both specific ("increase sales by 20%") and challenging (not too easy, not impossible) consistently outperform vague goals ("do your best") or easy goals.
- **Goal commitment matters** — the performance benefit depends on the person being committed to the goal, which is increased by the goal's importance and the person's self-efficacy.
- **Goals work through four mechanisms** — they direct attention, energize effort, encourage persistence, and promote the development of task-relevant strategies.
- **Feedback is necessary** — goals without feedback produce minimal performance gains; feedback allows people to track progress and adjust strategies.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "The highest performance is attained when goals are both specific and difficult." — the central finding

> "Goals affect performance through four mechanisms: they direct attention, energize, encourage persistence, and foster the development of task-relevant strategies." — on the mechanisms

## Notes / caveats

- This is a review paper synthesizing 35 years of goal-setting research, primarily from industrial/organizational psychology.
- The theory is most robust for tasks that are straightforward and require effort/attention; complex tasks may require additional support (e.g., learning goals, self-efficacy).
- The paper explicitly addresses the relationship between goal-setting and self-efficacy theory (Bandura), noting they are complementary, not competing.
- **Does NOT support:** that all goals are beneficial (impossible goals can be demotivating), or that goal-setting alone is sufficient (without commitment, feedback, and task-relevant strategies, goals don't work).
