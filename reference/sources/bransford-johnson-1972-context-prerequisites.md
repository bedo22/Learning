# Source digest — Bransford & Johnson, 1972

> Prior knowledge is a prerequisite for comprehension: without context activated *before* encoding, even simple material is barely remembered.

## What the doc(s) claim from this source

- learning-system claims: prior knowledge/schema activation before study dramatically improves comprehension and later recall — encoding depends on what you already hold.

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Context before beats context after** — participants who learned what an ambiguous passage was about *before* hearing it recalled roughly twice as many idea units as those told the topic afterwards, and several times more than those never told.
- **Comprehension drives recall, not exposure** — the passage was identical across groups; only whether prior knowledge was active during encoding differed.
- **The effect generalizes** — replicated across multiple passages and contexts (the "washing clothes" passage being the most famous).

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "Contextual prerequisites for understanding" — title framing: comprehension requires relevant prior knowledge to be active before input.

## Notes / caveats

- The stimuli are short artificial passages; the magnitude (~2×) should not be read as a universal constant for real coursework.
- **Does NOT support:** that prior knowledge substitutes for retrieval practice, or that telling students the topic once fixes all encoding problems — it shows activation of relevant knowledge at encoding is necessary for deep comprehension.
