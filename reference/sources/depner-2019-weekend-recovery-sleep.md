# Source digest — Depner et al., 2019

> Ad libitum weekend recovery sleep fails to prevent metabolic dysregulation:
> an in-lab experiment showing weekend catch-up sleep does not undo the costs
> of weekday sleep restriction.

## Identity

- **Full citation:** Depner, C. M., Melanson, E. L., Eckel, R. H., et al. (2019). Ad libitum Weekend Recovery Sleep Fails to Prevent Metabolic Dysregulation Induced by Insufficient Sleep during a Working Week. *Current Biology*, 29(6), 957–967.
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069
- **Verified link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069
- **Open-access link:** none (Cell press; PMC copy may exist)
- **First verified:** 2026-08-19
- **Last checked:** 2026-08-19

## What the doc(s) claim from this source

- rest-and-recovery claims (failure modes): "banking" sleep debt for the weekend —
  sleeping far longer on free days to repay weekday loss — does not repair the damage;
  stable timing beats catch-up.

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Recovery sleep is insufficient** — participants restricted to 5 h on work-nights then
  allowed unrestricted weekend sleep still showed metabolic dysregulation versus
  controls.
- **Catch-up worsened some outcomes** — the weekend-recovery group showed extra
  detriments (e.g., insulin sensitivity, weight gain markers) and recovered less on
  subsequent sleep-restricted nights.
- **Circadian disruption contributed** — irregular timing itself (late weekend nights)
  was part of the harm, consistent with social-jetlag theory.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "Ad libitum Weekend Recovery Sleep Fails to Prevent Metabolic Dysregulation" — title = finding.

## Notes / caveats

- In-lab study, healthy adults, ~2-week protocol, metabolic endpoints (not learning
  outcomes); generalization to memory consolidation is inferential.
- **Does NOT support:** that a single late night is damaging, or that recovery sleep has
  zero value — it shows catch-up does not fully reverse weekday restriction.
