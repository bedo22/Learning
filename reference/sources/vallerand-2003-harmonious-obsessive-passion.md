## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Two types of passion exist** — *harmonious passion* (the activity is freely accepted as part of the self; engagement is flexible) and *obsessive passion* (the activity controls the person; engagement is rigid and compulsive).
- **Harmonious passion predicts well-being** — people with harmonious passion show higher positive affect, flow experiences, and life satisfaction.
- **Obsessive passion predicts negative outcomes** — obsessive passion is associated with rigid persistence, interpersonal conflict, negative affect, and reduced performance under pressure.
- **Passion type mediates the relationship between activity and well-being** — it's not *whether* you're passionate that matters, but *what kind* of passion you have.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "Harmonious passion results from an autonomous internalization of the activity into one's identity... Obsessive passion results from a controlled internalization and corresponds to an internal compulsion to engage in the activity." — on the two types

> "Harmonious passion was positively related to positive affect, flow, and life satisfaction, whereas obsessive passion was negatively related to these variables." — key finding

## Notes / caveats

- The original study used correlational designs; later work (e.g., Vallerand, 2010) added longitudinal and experimental evidence.
- The model has been replicated across many activities (sports, music, work, education).
- **Does NOT support:** that passion is always good (obsessive passion has clear costs), or that you can "choose" which type of passion to have (passion type develops over time through internalization processes).
