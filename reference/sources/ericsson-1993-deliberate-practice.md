## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Expertise requires deliberate practice, not just experience** — the amount of deliberate practice (structured, effortful, with feedback) predicts expertise level more than years of experience.
- **Deliberate practice is effortful and not inherently enjoyable** — it involves working at the edge of one's ability, with immediate feedback, and is often not intrinsically motivating.
- **The ~10,000 hour rule (popular version)** — while Ericsson himself has disputed the oversimplified "10,000 hours" claim, the core finding is that extensive deliberate practice is necessary (though not sufficient) for expertise.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "The differences between expert performers and normal adults reflect a life-long period of deliberate effort to improve performance in a specific domain." — the core claim

> "Deliberate practice is not the most enjoyable activity, and it requires a high level of effort and concentration." — on the nature of the practice

## Notes / caveats

- This is a theoretical/empirical review paper, not a single experiment.
- The "deliberate practice" framework has been critiqued: Hambrick et al. (2014) argue that deliberate practice explains only a portion of performance variance (12–26% across domains), with genetic and other factors playing significant roles.
- The framework applies best to domains with clear performance standards and structured training (e.g., chess, music, sports). Its applicability to less structured domains (e.g., business, art) is debated.
- **Does NOT support:** that 10,000 hours guarantees expertise, or that talent/genetics play no role. The framework describes what practice is *necessary*, not what is *sufficient*.
