## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Tests improve retention more than restudying** — students who took a test after studying retained more after a one-week delay than students who restudied, even when the test had no feedback.
- **The testing effect is robust** — the benefit appeared across multiple experiments with different materials (word lists, prose passages).
- **The advantage grows with delay** — the testing effect was larger after a one-week delay than after five minutes, suggesting tests strengthen long-term storage, not just short-term performance.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "Students who read a passage and then took a test on the material retained more in the long term than did students who reread the passage." — abstract

> "Taking a test on material can enhance retention more than studying the material again." — key finding, Experiment 1

## Notes / caveats

- The original study used relatively simple materials (word lists, short prose passages). Subsequent meta-analyses (Rowland, 2014; Adesope et al., 2017) confirm the effect in classroom settings with more complex materials.
- The effect is about *retrieval practice* — the act of retrieving strengthens memory. Feedback helps but is not required for the basic effect.
- **Does NOT support:** that tests are the only way to learn, or that testing without any study produces good outcomes (the study-test sequence matters).
