## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Interleaving produces better learning than blocking** — students who mixed different types of math problems scored significantly higher on a surprise test than students who practiced one type at a time.
- **The benefit appears on delayed tests** — interleaving helped most when students had to identify which strategy to apply (a discrimination task), not just execute a known strategy.
- **The effect is robust** — replicated across multiple experiments with middle-school students.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "Students who received interleaved practice scored 72% on the surprise test, compared to 38% for blocked practice." — the key finding

## Notes / caveats

- The effect is strongest for tasks that require *discrimination* between problem types (e.g., choosing the right math formula), less strong for tasks that just require execution.
- Interleaving is harder in the moment — students feel like they're learning less — but they actually learn more. This is another desirable difficulty.
- **Does NOT support:** that interleaving is always better (for simple skill acquisition, blocking can be more efficient), or that interleaving eliminates the need for initial instruction.
