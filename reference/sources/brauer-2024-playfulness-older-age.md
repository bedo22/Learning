# Source digest — Brauer, Stumpf & Proyer, 2024

> "Playfulness in middle- and older age: testing associations with life

## What the doc(s) claim from this source

- play-as-recovery claims: playfulness does not decline much with age — the correlation between age and playfulness is only r = -.13 (Proyer, 2014, cited in this paper).
- play-as-recovery claims: the OLIW model of adult playfulness has four facets — Other-directed, Lighthearted, Intellectual, Whimsical.
- play-as-recovery claims: Other-directed playfulness is the facet most associated with positive psychological functioning.
- play-as-recovery claims: playfulness relates to life satisfaction, well-being (PERMA domains), and character strengths with small-to-medium effect sizes.

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Playfulness declines only slightly with age** — r = -.13 across 4100 participants aged 18–92: "Proyer's (2014b) study of 4100 participants between 18 and 92 years of age showed a minor association of .13 between age and a unidimensional measure of playfulness."
- **Playfulness expressions in older adults are comparable to younger adults** — "The playfulness expressions in this age group were comparable to younger adults."
- **Other-directed playfulness is the key facet** — most associated with positive functioning: "regression analyses revealing that particularly Other-directed is positively associated with positive psychological functioning."
- **Playfulness relates to life satisfaction, well-being, and character strengths** — small-to-medium effects: "We found that playfulness relates to life satisfaction, the PERMA domains of well-being, and character strengths with small-to-medium correlation effect sizes."
- **Playfulness can be trained** — Proyer et al. (2021) RCT showed 1-week training increased playfulness and well-being, effects lasting 12 weeks: "participants trained their playfulness with short homework-like tasks... increases in playfulness predicted greater well-being and alleviated depressive symptoms."
- **Playfulness benefits relationships across all ages** — including couples aged 50–98: "playfulness is important for relationship satisfaction across all ages, including couples between the age of 50 and 98 years."

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "We don't stop playing because we grow old; we grow old because we stop playing." — George Bernard Shaw, cited in the paper's introduction

> "playfulness may act as a resource for coping with challenges and life events common in later life, such as the transition from work to retirement, bereavement, or health issues" — on playfulness as aging resource

> "Other-directed (i.e. using one's playfulness to cheer others up and solve social tension), Lighthearted (i.e. seeing life as a game and liking to improvise instead of planning ahead), Intellectual (i.e. preference for complexity over simplicity, liking to play with ideas), and Whimsical (i.e. preference for odd and/or unusual things, persons or activities)" — the OLIW facets

## Notes / caveats

- Cross-sectional design — age associations are correlational, not longitudinal. The minor age decline could reflect cohort effects, not aging per se.
- The OLIW model is Proyer's (2017) measure — different from other playfulness scales. The four facets provide granularity that a single "playfulness" score misses.
- The Proyer et al. (2021) RCT cited in this paper is the key intervention evidence: playfulness can be trained with simple exercises (listing 3 playful things per day for 7 days).
- **Does NOT support:** that playfulness is immune to aging, or that training playfulness is equivalent to play-as-recovery. The paper shows playfulness relates to well-being; the recovery mechanism is separate (REQ experiences).
