# Source digest — Wilson et al., 2014 (alone with thoughts)

> "Just Think: The Challenges of the Disengaged Mind" (Science) — the
> infamous "people shocked themselves rather than sit alone with thoughts"
> study. Cited in rest-and-recovery for why doing nothing is hard.

## Identity

- **Full citation:** Wilson, T. D., Reinhard, D. A., Westgate, E. C., Gilbert, D. T., Ellerbeck, N., Hahn, C., Brown, C. L., & Shaked, A. (2014). Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind. *Science*, 345(6192), 75–77.
- **DOI:** 10.1126/science.1250830
- **Verified link:** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1250830
- **Open-access link:** none reliably found (Science paywalled)

## What the doc(s) claim from this source

- rest-and-recovery claims: "In the classic study, people were alone with only their thoughts for 6–15 minutes; on average the activity was mildly unpleasant ... some participants preferred to give themselves mild electric shocks rather than sit quietly (Wilson et al., Science, 2014)."
- rest-and-recovery claims: "The brain evolved to track external things; switching inward is learnable but feels uncomfortable at first (Wilson et al.)."

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Being alone with your thoughts is mildly unpleasant on average** — participants scored the alone-with-thoughts period low and found it aversive.
- **Many preferred sensory stimulation/even electric shock to silence** — the doc's shock anecdote; in the most-discussed condition a substantial fraction chose shock over "just thinking."
- **Disengagement is hard because the mind resists doing nothing** — the doc uses this to explain why people reach for entertainment to keep thoughts out.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "Don't leave me alone with my thoughts ... it appears to be an unpleasant experience. In fact, many of the people studied, particularly the men, chose to give themselves a mild electric shock rather than be deprived of external sensory stimuli" — Science summary (editor-preview of Wilson et al.)

## Notes / caveats

- **The headline is oversold:** the doc itself carries the rebuttal (see fox-2014-is-thinking-aversive.md) — reactions were variable, "just thinking" was rated roughly neutral-to-somewhat-enjoyable, and there was no statistical difference in enjoyment between shockers and non-shockers. The doc presents both the finding AND the closer read.
- Shocking rates were strongly sex-skewed (mostly men) and shockers were a subset, not the majority of all conditions.
- **Does NOT support:** that idleness is always aversive or that everyone finds it so — the doc's disputed box makes this explicit.
