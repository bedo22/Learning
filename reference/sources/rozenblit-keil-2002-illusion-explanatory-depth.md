## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **People overestimate how well they understand everyday mechanisms** — when asked to rate their understanding of zippers, toilets, or helicopters, people give high ratings. When asked to produce a step-by-step explanation, ratings plummet.
- **The illusion is specific to explanation** — people can accurately judge whether they can *use* something (e.g., a toilet), but they confuse usage knowledge with understanding of *how it works*.
- **The effect is robust** — it appears across many domains and is not simply a demand characteristic.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "People feel they understand mechanical, biological, and social phenomena more deeply than they actually do." — the core finding

## Notes / caveats

- The effect is about *explanatory* knowledge, not *procedural* knowledge. People really do know how to use things; they just don't know how they work.
- The study used a "prediction-explanation" paradigm: rate understanding → try to explain → re-rate.
- **Does NOT support:** that all self-assessment is unreliable (people are accurate about many things), or that the illusion is unique to any one domain.
