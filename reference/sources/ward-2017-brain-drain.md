## What the doc(s) claim from this source

- wanting-vs-liking claims: "add friction (grayscale, no autoplay, physical distance — the mere presence of the phone drains cognitive capacity, Ward et al., 2017)."
- Also referenced as an "application" citation in the doc's reference span.

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **A visible smartphone drains working-memory capacity even when not used** — the mere presence of one's own phone reduces available cognitive resources, supporting the "brain drain" hypothesis, even while attention is sustained.
- **The costs are largest for the most smartphone-dependent** — dependence moderates the drain.
- **The cognitive cost is ongoing, not only during use** — this powers the doc's "friction / distance" fix (reducing cue *and* presence cost).

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "results from two experiments indicate that even when people are successful at maintaining sustained attention—as when avoiding the temptation to check their phones—the mere presence of these devices reduces available cognitive capacity. Moreover, these cognitive costs are highest for those highest in smartphone dependence" — abstract

## Notes / caveats

- Note: a later meta-analysis (e.g., Parry et al., 2022) found the "mere presence" effect is smaller than the original papers suggested; the doc uses it as an illustrative reason for physical distance, not a strictly decisive effect.
- **Does NOT support:** a large, universal brain-drain effect size; treat the presence-cost as real but modest, the doc's "add friction" advice is about cue-strength more than drain alone.
