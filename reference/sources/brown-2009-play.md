## What the doc(s) claim from this source

- play-as-recovery claims: Brown's definition — play is "an ancient, voluntary, inherently pleasurable, apparently purposeless activity or process that is undertaken for its own sake."
- play-as-recovery claims: play has seven properties — (1) apparently purposeless, (2) voluntary, (3) inherent attraction, (4) freedom from time, (5) diminished consciousness of self, (6) improvisational potential, (7) continuation desire.
- play-as-recovery claims: the 7 properties are *graded*, not a checklist (via Zosh et al. 2018's reading of Brown).

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Play = activity done for its own sake** — supported by Brown's definition quote below ("undertaken for its own sake").
- **"Apparently purposeless" means no external outcome as the aim, NOT no meaning** — supported by the "apparently purposeless" qualifier; this is the doc's key clarification target.
- **The 7 properties are graded, not a required checklist** — supported via Zosh et al. 2018's reading; only the first three (purposeless, voluntary, inherently attractive) are load-bearing.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "an ancient, voluntary, inherently pleasurable, apparently purposeless activity or process that is undertaken for its own sake" — Brown's definition (also reproduced in Zosh et al., 2018, Definitions section)

> Seven properties listed verbatim in Zosh et al., 2018: "apparently purposeless/done for its own sake, voluntary, inherent attraction, freedom from time, diminished consciousness of self, improvisational potential, and continuation desire."

## Notes / caveats

- The book itself is the source of the definition + 7 properties; the doc's exact list was verified through Zosh et al. 2018 (which quotes Brown 2010 edition) and the 6,000-play-history claim traces to Brown's work/NIFP.
- The "apparently purposeless" property is the doc's key clarification target: it means *no external outcome as the aim*, not *no meaning*.
- **Does NOT support:** empirical dosing of play, recovery science, or the recovery-experience taxonomy — those come from Sonnentag/Burghardt elsewhere. Brown is the conceptual/definitional anchor, not an empirical recovery source.
