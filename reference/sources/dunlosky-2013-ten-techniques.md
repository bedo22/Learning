# Source digest — Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham, 2013

> The definitive large-scale review of 10 study techniques: practice testing and distributed practice rank highest; rereading, highlighting, and summarization rank lowest. The evidence base for the shelf's study-advice doc.

## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Practice testing and distributed practice are the two most effective techniques** — both have extensive evidence across lab and classroom settings, and work across age groups and content types.
- **Rereading and highlighting are low-value** — despite being the most popular techniques among students, they produce minimal improvement in long-term retention.
- **The evidence varies by technique** — some techniques (e.g., interleaving) have moderate evidence that is growing; others (e.g., summarization) have mixed results depending on how they're implemented.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "Practice testing (self-testing or taking practice tests) and distributed practice (implementing a schedule of practice that spreads study activities out over time) enhance student learning and are applicable in a variety of educational contexts." — abstract

> "Rereading and highlighting did not show strong evidence of utility." — on the least effective techniques

## Notes / caveats

- This is a review paper, not a single experiment. It synthesizes hundreds of studies.
- The ranking is based on evidence *quality* and *generalizability*, not effect size alone.
- The paper explicitly notes that even low-ranked techniques may be useful as *supplements* to high-ranked techniques (e.g., highlighting can help identify what to test yourself on).
- **Does NOT support:** that any technique is useless, or that students should abandon all low-ranked techniques entirely. The paper's practical advice is to prioritize high-ranked techniques.
