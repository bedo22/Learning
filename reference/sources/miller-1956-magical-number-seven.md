## Key findings (one line each, with supporting quote)

- **Short-term memory capacity is approximately 7±2 items** — people can hold about 5–9 "chunks" of information in immediate memory simultaneously.
- **Chunks are flexible units** — an item can be a digit, a word, or a meaningful unit; chunking (grouping items into larger units) effectively increases capacity.
- **The limit applies across domains** — the same 7±2 limit appears in digit span, letter span, absolute judgment, and subitizing.

## Key quotes (with locations)

> "The number of objects an average human can hold in immediate memory is seven plus or minus two." — the famous summary

> "The span of immediate memory seems to be limited by the number of chunks, not the number of items." — on the flexibility of chunks

## Notes / caveats

- Miller himself noted the paper was partly tongue-in-cheek: "My original argument is that my argument is an elaborate poke in the ribs."
- The 7±2 estimate applies to immediate memory (short-term/working memory), not long-term memory.
- Cowan (2001) later argued the true capacity is closer to 4 chunks, not 7 — the difference depends on whether chunking is allowed.
- **Does NOT support:** that you can remember exactly 7 things, or that capacity cannot be improved (chunking strategies effectively increase it).
