Glossary

Shared, canonical terms for the whole course — adhered to in every reference & lesson

This glossary grows as we process more transcripts. Terms here are canonical: use them consistently everywhere. If a later transcript refines a term, we update it here and note the change.

Bloom's revised taxonomy framework
A six-level hierarchy of cognitive thinking — Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create. Published 1956, revised 2001. Built for educators and exam writers; most curricula are designed around it.
Thinking level principle
Justin Sung's split of each Bloom level into the thinking you do (e.g. Evaluate) and the result it unlocks (e.g. Prioritize). See Bloom's Six Levels of Thinking.
Remember → Regurgitate level 1
Rote learning (rereading, rewriting, mass flashcards). Output: listing, defining, stating facts. Weak retention.
Understand → Explain level 2
Comprehend meaning. Same technique as L1, different intent. Output: explaining a concept.
Apply → Solve level 3
Use one learned concept to solve a "one-to-one" problem.
Analyze → Compare level 4
Compare & contrast; see information in relation to other information. The first higher-order level.
Evaluate → Prioritize level 5
Form and justify a conclusion ("so what? why does it matter?"). The top-learner level.
Create → Generate level 6
Produce something new. Advanced; most people peak at level 5.
Top-down studying principle
Start at level 5 (Evaluate) and work down, instead of climbing 1→5. Higher levels build stronger memory; lower-level results arrive as side effects.
Knowledge decay / forgetting curve principle
Memory fades over time — the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve. Spaced retrieval flattens it each pass; better encoding flattens it from the start (good encoding → review every few weeks, not days). The reason bottom-up climbing fails (you keep relearning what you forgot).
Misinterpreted effort hypothesis principle
Mistaking the effort of higher-order thinking for "doing it wrong," causing learners to quit and never improve. The effort is the mechanism that builds storage strength.
One-to-one problem term
A problem solvable with exactly one learned concept (level 3).
Learning system framework
See learning as a system with two components — encoding and retrieval — not a loose pile of techniques. When something's off, find which component is weak and fix that.
Encoding component
Turning new information into memory — finding patterns, angles, connections so the brain sees it as relevant. The most life-changing skill to improve, but takes months–years to rewire. See The Learning System.
Retrieval component
Pulling stored memory out and using it (explain, solve, answer). Re-encodes as it goes — a cycle. Quick to learn, pays off immediately, so start here before encoding.
Spaced retrieval technique
Test yourself after meaningful forgetting, then re-encode; each pass flattens the decay curve. ("Spaced repetition" is the imprecise common term — retrieval is the active part.) Starter template: 1 day → 1 week → 1 month, then adjust by measuring retention.
Flashcard overwhelm pitfall
Poor encoding → steep decay → you must review every 2–3 days or lose it → hundreds of daily cards. Pushing a boulder uphill; miss a day and it rolls back. Fixed by better encoding, not more cards.
Recognition / cued recall / free recall spectrum
Retrieval methods, weak → strong. Recognition (flashcard flip, MCQ) — weakest; can show 90% retention yet only ~50% functional ability. Cued recall (fill-in-the-blank) — medium. Free recall ("solve this from everything you know", no cues) — strongest.
Generative retrieval principle
Do something with the knowledge — make a question, solve, explain, write — and manipulate it (combine facts: "now that I know X, what does that mean for Y?"). Free recall + generative + manipulative = the strongest retrieval.
Opportunistic vs deliberate retrieval principle
Deliberate = a separate scheduled session (extra work). Opportunistic = fold retrieval into work you already do (teach the team briefing from memory). For professionals, opportunistic first; deliberate only for knowledge not used daily.
Orders of learning framework
Justin Sung's 2-level model of how integrated knowledge is — the practical "how deep do I go?" goalpost. Pairs with Bloom's six levels (higher-order ≈ Bloom's upper levels). See Orders of Learning.
Higher order / lower order principle
Lower = isolated facts/concepts (fast decay). Higher = integrated; a concept matters via its influence on other things. Higher-order forces info into a schema → stronger memory → flatter decay.
Schema (network) term
A connected structure of related knowledge. Memory is stronger when information belongs inside a network/schema — the mechanism by which higher-order learning flattens decay.
Mind mapping technique
Effective only when done correctly — genuinely evaluate, prioritize, restructure to find the best connections. Just drawing boxes and arrows without deep thought is ineffective ("mind mapping without higher-order thinking").
Analogy technique
An encoding tool: re-represent related concepts as something else, then test "does this still make sense?" — the test forces you to verify each piece. Use frequently when learning new information. Go further: critique your analogies — refining them ("life is like a journey" → "a spontaneous journey through a foggy forest") forces deeper examination and higher cognitive load. The critiqued analogy wins 99/100. See Study Advice.
Feynman method technique
Teach it to a (real or imaginary) 10-year-old, from memory, in the simplest terms. Higher-order + generative + free recall at once. Prioritizes, restructures, and checks accuracy.
Principle over tactic principle
Any method works if it forces thinking in networks/relationships. You don't have to use mind maps, analogies, or Feynman — the principle (think in relationships, not isolated facts) matters more than the specific tactic.
Time myth / IQ myth myth
"I don't have enough time / I'm not smart enough." Being 20–30% more effective frees 20–30% of your time — the fix is how you learn, not more hours or better genes.
Learning styles vs learning preferences myth
"I have a learning style" is debunked (the field now studies why people still believe it). We do have preferences — but they're a starting point, not a fixed ceiling; mixed-modal learning helps everyone.
Counterproductive adjustment pitfall
Abandoning an effective strategy for an ineffective one because the effective one feels harder. Driven by the misinterpreted effort hypothesis.
Self-management domain
Managing time, priorities, focus & attention — what gives you the time/energy/concentration to do effective learning. Three parts: time, tasks, focus. See Self-Management.
Under-scheduling principle
Plan to do less than you'd like. Over-scheduling = always behind and guilty; under-scheduling is honest and plannable. Track your time to see how long things actually take.
Eisenhower matrix tool
Sort tasks by urgent × important. Measure importance by the consequence of not doing it, not by feeling (the brain feels urgency, not long-term importance).
Urgency trap pitfall
Doing only urgent tasks and never the important-but-not-urgent ones (health, skills). By the time those become urgent, it's too late.
Procrastination (emotional) principle
A coping mechanism for the discomfort of anticipated effort — you pick an easier path to dodge the feeling. The distractions aren't the problem; the avoidance is. Block one distraction and you'll find another.
Accountability (identity-based) tool
A partner/group beats reward/consequence apps because it swaps fragile dopamine-based motivation for durable identity-based motivation ("I'm someone who keeps my word").
Zeigarnik effect principle
The brain likes to finish what's started. Most anguish is in starting; once started, continuing is far easier. Tactic: when resistance hits, just start (open tabs, scaffold) — don't aim to finish.
Mindfulness (get good at being bored) practice
Long-term focus training: attend fully to anything (breathing, walking, dishes) and each time the mind wanders, return. Boring breathing is best — maximum wandering = maximum reps of returning. Being okay with boredom is the training; it transfers to workday focus.
Memory handling (vs capacity) principle
The reframe: you don't have a bad memory, you have poor memory handling. Memory champions use strategies, not better brains. What you do with new info determines whether it's stored — not the "quality" of your memory. Handling is trainable. See Memory Handling.
Working memory (= short-term memory) term
The workbench where you mold new info. Lasts ~15–30 seconds (not hours or a day — most "bad short-term memory" is actually rapid long-term decay). Has a time limit (15–30s) and a weight limit (~5–9 items). What you do in the 15–30s window dictates long-term storage quality.
Working memory weight limit principle
~5–9 items (Ebbinghaus: ~7; Miller 1956); modern lab estimates ~4 chunks (Cowan 2001). But an "item" is fuzzy: a pattern collapses items (123456789 = 1 item); prior knowledge collapses items ("serotonin syndrome" = 1 item to a medic, 2 to a layperson). Patterns and prior knowledge lighten the load.
Cognitive overload pitfall
When too much enters working memory at once, it exceeds the weight limit and the brain produces no effective output. Prevent it with a stop-start approach: pause consuming, handle one thing, then let the next in.
Handle immediately principle
Handle new info within the 15–30s window of first encountering it — don't "consume first, make sense later" (that assumes understanding = memory; it doesn't). Ten extra seconds now saves relearning time later.
Cognitive offload (think on paper) technique
Justin Sung's favorite strategy. Write/draw on paper so working memory doesn't waste resources holding info — it just looks at the paper and focuses on the current connection. The best use of note-taking for learning.
Retrieval failure term
The memory is stored in long-term memory — you just can't pull it out. Distinct from bad encoding. Fixed by practicing retrieval at the fluency level and with the cues you'll actually face (flashcard cues don't transfer to complex questions).
Studying vs learning principle
Studying = the activity (notes, reading, testing). Learning = the outcome — only happened if you can remember, understand deeply, and apply. Methods sit on a spectrum of effectiveness (rereading/highlighting ≈ no learning). See Study Advice.
Deep processing principle
The trainable ability that separates top learners — not genetics. Copying what successful students do fails because the difference is how they process. Same muscle as higher-order thinking.
Toolbox (techniques as tools) principle
No perfect technique exists. Each is a tool with a purpose and limits — use it for what it's good for and supplement. Classic abuse: flashcards for everything. See Study Advice.
Illusion of learning pitfall
Doing something that feels like learning but isn't (e.g. 3 hours rewriting notes you can't recall). The learning equivalent of productive procrastination. Test: "if examined right now, how would I do?" See Study Advice.
Declarative vs procedural knowledge principle
Declarative = the what (concepts, definitions). Procedural = the how (executing, solving, doing). Most subjects need both; test both in the balance your subject demands. See Study Advice.
Silly mistake syndrome pitfall
Blaming losses on "silly mistakes" when they're real knowledge gaps. If you couldn't generate the answer — even if the answer sheet makes sense — there's a gap. Assume every mistake is real. See Study Advice.
Priming / scoping technique
Before learning a subject, get a big-picture overview (main ideas, how they connect) without memorizing details. Gives the brain context & relevance — the "why/where" before the "what." High correlation with learning. Library analogy: learn the shelves before books arrive. See Study Advice.
Memory palace / link method technique
Proven rote-memorization techniques (method of loci; linking). Very effective for their purpose — last-resort use when rote memorization is genuinely needed. Don't abuse beyond it. See Study Advice.
Learning log practice
Track what you're testing, what you think will help, what technique you're trying, how it went, what's next. You can only change 1–2 things at a time (biological limit); a log prevents losing track and shows invisible progress. Justin Sung estimates it would have cut 3 years → 9 months. See Study Advice.
Rest vs recovery principle
Rest (not doing much) is not the same as recovery. Recovery requires relaxation — low activity for the system you're recovering (physical → low physical; cognitive → low cognitive). Filling "rest" with low-effort-but-attention-demanding tasks (social media, scrolling) is fake rest — it makes you more tired. See Rest & Recovery.
Four dimensions of recovery framework
Sonnentag & Fritz: (1) relaxation — low activity for the recovering system; (2) psychological detachment — mentally switching off from work/stressors; (3) mastery — learning something with a sense of progress (compounds into a relaxation asset); (4) control — spending time the way you choose. See Rest & Recovery.
Psychological detachment principle
Mentally switching off from work & stressors — not thinking about them, not checking messages "just this once," not planning tomorrow. People who detach report more life satisfaction, less burnout, and are just as engaged at work.
Recovery paradox pitfall
People recover the least when they need it the most — high workload makes detachment harder. The exhausted are the least able to access recovery.
Mastery (recovery) principle
A recovery dimension: learning something new with a sense of progress (gym, dance, puzzle, language). Triggers detachment + relaxation, and compounds — as you improve, it becomes a permanent relaxation asset. The high effort is what produces the recovery.
Prefrontal cortex fatigue principle
As the day goes on, the prefrontal cortex (decision-making, planning, self-regulation) fatigues and can't make high-effort, planning-heavy decisions — so you default to couch-potato "rest" even when you know recovery activities would help. The tiredness is lying: you're exhausted because you lack recovery, not because you can't handle it.
Soft fascination (nature) principle
A mechanism observed only with nature: you're effortlessly engaged by the environment, which renews attention, energy, and working-memory capacity. Closest thing to a recovery "magic pill." Guideline: ~30 min in one session, or ~120 min/week cumulative.
Productivity (defined) principle
Stuff you do that gets you toward your goals — not busy, not more things, not time management, unless those serve your outcomes. You decide what's meaningful (a chill life is valid). Refine to 2–3 outcomes. See Productivity Systems.
Flow matching technique
Match a task's required concentration/energy to the energy state you'll be in at that time of day. Deep work into cognitive peaks; low-concentration tasks into troughs. Lets you work fewer hours at higher quality. See Productivity Systems #2.
Scripted actions (if-then) technique
Predetermined "if X then Y" decisions for high-risk tasks, made upfront when the prefrontal cortex is fresh. Halve the intention–behavior gap (~2× more likely to follow through) — because at execution time the fatigued PFC only executes, doesn't decide. See Productivity Systems #3.
Action architecture (cues) principle
Engineer your environment with cues (triggers that prompt a behavior) rather than relying on motivation (only 20–30% of action). Add good cues, remove distracting cues; iterate for weeks. The shift from motivation-dependent to cue-dependent productivity. See Productivity Systems #4.
Cue–response pairing principle
A habit requires a very clear, specific cue → response. Complex goals ("be healthier") are webs of decisions and can't become habits; specific ones ("when I see the pull-up bar, I do 5 pull-ups") can. Clearer pairing = faster habit. Once habitual, it runs on a different brain region and needs no PFC energy. See Productivity Systems #4.
Chronic PFC fatigue pitfall
When the prefrontal cortex never recovers — you can't remember feeling energized. From inadequate recovery, and it doesn't necessarily recover with sleep. Fixed by the four recovery dimensions, not just more sleep.
Reverse goal setting method
Start with the goal and work backwards to a clear path, via 5 steps: define the goal, define your future self (meta-goal), define your current self, force field analysis, build the plan. Fixes normal goal setting (which focuses on the what, not the how). See Reverse Goal Setting.
Meta-goal principle
Not the outcome — the person for whom achieving the goal would be easy. The skills, attributes, habits, and opportunities that person has. Becoming that person becomes your #1 goal, because outcomes are symptoms of the process you control.
Process vs outcome principle
Outcomes can't be controlled — they're symptoms of the process you engage in. So channel goal-worry into evaluating your processes and skills, not obsessing over the outcome. (Lottery winners lose the money because they have the outcome but not the process.)
Force field analysis tool
Examine the forces pushing you toward or holding you back from a goal: barriers to overcome, resources/opportunities already accessible. Revisit every few months. Step 4 of reverse goal setting.
Bottleneck principle principle
The real blocker is often upstream of where you think — e.g. you think you need better learning skills, but the actual blocker is an unsafe home environment or procrastination. Step back and evaluate holistically before assuming the obvious gap is the one to fix first.
RAIL framework method
Four stages of learning any complex skill: Relevance (know what to care about) → Awareness (try & make mistakes; experiment + reflect + get feedback) → Iteration (perform correctly but inconsistently; varied practice + adjust; consistency before speed) → Lifelong (habitual; refine + regular use or skill decay). More practical than the four stages of competence it's derived from. See Skill Acquisition.
Four stages of competence framework
Unconscious incompetence → conscious incompetence → conscious competence → unconscious competence. The classic model RAIL is derived from; dense and abstract — RAIL adds the actions to take at each stage.
Latent learning principle
Learning with no immediate reinforcement (Tolman, 1930s). Simple skills give instant feedback (shoelaces); complex skills give delayed, ambiguous feedback — this latent period is what makes them hard and why people give up.
Skill decay pitfall
Neglected skills slip back to earlier RAIL stages. Stage 4 (Lifelong) requires regular use to maintain — "if you don't use it you will lose it."
Theory–practice balance principle
Rule of thumb: ~1 hour theory : 5 hours practice. Balance new theory coming in with existing skills becoming habits (which frees cognitive resources). Too much theory without practice = overload; practice without theory = aimless.
Three learning pillars framework
Justin Sung's ordered progression: (1) enablers (time/task/focus — Self-Management), (2) retrieval, (3) encoding. Master them in that order — you can't have consistent retrieval without enablers, and encoding (the biggest prize) takes longest so comes last.
Expert mindset principle
Learn with the context/purpose of an expert, not just to hit current requirements — the brain stores information differently depending on the purpose, so aiming higher consolidates knowledge closer to an expert's.
"Don't overeat" mental check principle
Two questions to ask while learning: (1) "Does this make sense?" (2) "Do I feel like I'm about to forget this?" If either is no, stop consuming and let your brain process. "You don't have to finish the bag of chips just because you opened it."
Wanting vs liking principle
Berridge & Robinson's split of reward into three separable components: wanting (dopamine-driven incentive salience, a cue-triggered craving that runs without enjoyment), liking (opioid-driven hedonic impact, actual pleasure during), and learning (cue associations). The two systems can dissociate: doomscrolling is high wanting, low liking; "I enjoy it once I start" is high liking, low wanting. Both play and study run on the liking meter — the urge is the wrong signal. See Wanting vs Liking (full treatment) and Play as Recovery (recovery application).
Incentive salience term
The technical name for wanting: the cue-triggered "attractiveness" of a reward that makes its cues compelling, whether or not you enjoy the reward itself. The mechanism by which a phone buzz becomes attractive. See Wanting vs Liking.
Hedonic hotspot term
A small brain site where opioid/endocannabinoid activity produces pleasure (nucleus accumbens shell, ventral pallidum, orbitofrontal cortex) — tiny compared with the dopamine projection, which is why liking is fragile while wanting is loud. See Wanting vs Liking.
Anhedonia clinical flag
Collapsed liking: everything is flat for two-plus weeks, including things you used to love. A depression flag, not a play or discipline problem. See Wanting vs Liking.
Apathy clinical flag
Collapsed wanting: nothing pulls you, even things you'd enjoy (seen in Parkinson's and some depression) — the start problem at its extreme. See Wanting vs Liking.
Wanting-feed pitfall
Any interface engineered for non-satiation — infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks, gacha — wanting with no built-in liking. The algorithm gets the reward (your engagement), not you. See Wanting vs Liking and Play as Recovery.
Desirable difficulties principle
Bjork's term for the effortful conditions (spacing, retrieval, interleaving, testing) that slow initial learning but produce stronger long-term retention and transfer — while feeling like they're not working. The science behind "learning should feel hard." See Study Advice.
Eudaimonic entertainment term
Meaningful media — stories that move or deepen you rather than merely amuse (hedonic). Both recover vitality via different routes (Rieger et al., 2014). Legitimate play when meaning is experienced; work when demanded as an outcome. See Play as Recovery.
Wakeful rest term
Brief quiet time after learning — no input, no task, no screens — during which the brain consolidates what was just studied (Dewar et al., 2012; Weng et al., 2025). A cheap memory booster: 10–15 minutes of stillness after a study session. See Rest & Recovery.
Zero-outcome container tool
A scheduled block of time with no goals, no stats, no optimization — the container for play. You schedule the space, not the content; obligation kills play, so the slot is fixed and the activity inside is free. See Play as Recovery.
Fluency illusion pitfall
The feeling that material is learned because it felt easy or smooth to consume — the mechanism behind rereading, highlighting, and AI-generated answers. Fluency is a poor proxy for learning; test recall, not comfort. See Study Advice · AI and learning.
Metacognition principle
Thinking about your own learning — monitoring (judging what you know) and control (deciding what to do about it). Control is only as good as monitoring: a miscalibrated monitor makes every study decision inherit the error. See Metacognition & Calibration.
Calibration principle
The match between what you predict you know and what you actually perform. People are systematically overconfident after study (judgments of learning run above performance). The cure is measurement, not introspection: test, then compare. See Metacognition & Calibration.
Self-determination theory (three needs) principle
Deci & Ryan: intrinsic motivation thrives on autonomy (you chose it), competence (you feel effective), and relatedness (you feel connected). Each maps to study design — check the three dials before blaming yourself. See Motivation & Self-Determination.
Overjustification effect pitfall
Rewarding an activity people already enjoy drops their intrinsic motivation for it — the activity becomes a means to the reward. Bribing yourself through a subject you could enjoy converts it into a paid chore. See Motivation & Self-Determination.
Implementation intentions / if-then plans tool
"If situation X, then I do Y" — pre-deciding the response to a trigger roughly doubles follow-through on intended behavior (Gollwitzer, 1999). The play doc's state triggers and WOOP's plan step are the same mechanism. See Motivation & Self-Determination, Play as Recovery, and Planning & Execution.
WOOP / mental contrasting tool
Oettingen's follow-through method: Wish → Outcome → Obstacle → Plan. You contrast the desired future with the inner obstacle, then write an if-then plan for that specific obstacle. The contrast step is the active ingredient. See Motivation & Self-Determination.
ICAP framework principle
Chi & Wylie's ranking of learning behaviors: Interactive (highest — dialoguing, explaining to others) > Constructive (producing new output — own words, connections) > Active (manipulating given material — highlighting, copying) > Passive (receiving — reading, listening). Re-reading notes is passive; quizzing from them is interactive. See Note-taking.
Self-explanation tool
Explaining material to yourself — "why does this step follow? what would break if I changed it?" — simultaneously improves learning and exposes gaps (Chi, 2000). It converts the illusion of explanatory depth into detectable holes. See Metacognition & Calibration.
Region of proximal learning principle
Metcalfe & Kornell: you study most efficiently when focused on items that are almost — but not quite — mastered. Items already mastered waste time (labor-in-vain); items too far beyond you lack the hooks to stick. See Metacognition & Calibration.
Sensitization principle
Repeated exposure amplifies the wanting system's response to cues — so a feed or app gets more compelling over time even as the experience stays flat. Why removing the cue (not enduring it) is the durable fix. See Wanting vs Liking.
Cold reproduction tool
Producing the answer with the tool closed — the liking test applied to study. If you can't reproduce it without the AI or the notes, it wasn't yours. See Wanting vs Liking and AI & Learning.
Extinction principle
The only mechanism that removes a conditioned urge: repeated exposure to the cue without the reward. Suppression never removes — it rebounds; extinction does. See Wanting vs Liking.
Urge surfing practice
Noticing the urge and letting it peak and pass without acting on it (acceptance with non-action). Observing the urge without obeying it weakens it; fighting it feeds it. See Wanting vs Liking.
Intention–action gap principle
The gap between intending to do something and actually doing it. Goals alone predict behavior weakly; the gap closes when the intention is specified as an if-then plan (implementation intentions) with a when, a where, and a first tiny step. See Planning & Execution.
Planning fallacy pitfall
The replicated error of underestimating how long tasks take and overestimating follow-through — planning from the inside view (the imagined best path) instead of the outside view (the base rate of how long similar tasks actually took). Fix: use the base rate, add margin, under-schedule, and review plan-vs-actual weekly. See Planning & Execution.
Attention residue principle
When you switch tasks, part of your attention stays on the previous one — the larger the unfinished task, the more residue bleeds into the new task (Leroy, 2009). The phone-check mid-study is not a break; it leaves residue that degrades the study after you return. See Focus & Attention and Self-Management.
Mind-wandering principle
Task-unrelated thought — the brain's default state and the leak that turns "study hours" into no learning. Also a signal: boredom (task too easy), overwhelm (task too hard), or an unmet need. Fix by signal: harder task, decomposed task, capture-on-paper, or rest. See Focus & Attention.
