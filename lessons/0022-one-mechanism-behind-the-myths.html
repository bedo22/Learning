One Mechanism Behind the Myths

Why every learning myth feels true — and how to tell

Learning styles, easy learning, "I'm just not smart enough," multitasking, brain training — they look like eight different mistakes. They're one mistake wearing eight costumes: fluency feels like learning. This lesson gives you the single test that dissolves all of them.

The costume shop

MythWhy it feels trueThe fluency illusion inside it
"I have a learning style"You have preferencesPreferring a format ≠ learning from it; matching style to instruction shows almost no benefit.
"Learning should feel easy"Easy feels smoothRereading feels fluent and produces almost no learning — the classic fluency trap.
"I'm not smart enough"Effort feels like failureStruggle is the desirable difficulty; the feeling of struggle is the feeling of learning.
"Multitasking works"Switching is fastFast switching feels busy; attention residue + task-switch costs mean nothing actually got learned.
"Brain training makes me smarter"Games feel like growthYou improve at the trained task; no reliable far transfer to intelligence.

The one test

Every myth asks you to judge by how it feels. The test is to judge by what you can produce. Ask: "Can I generate the answer from memory, with the material closed?" Fluency fails this test every time; learning passes it. If you felt it "click" but can't produce it, that wasn't learning — that was recognition wearing a graduation gown.

Spotting a myth in the wild

  • It explains by feeling ("it just clicked," "this feels right").
  • It promises a shortcut that bypasses retrieval (train the brain, match the style, think positive).
  • It makes you feel good about not testing yourself.

When a claim checks all three, reach for the production test before believing it.
