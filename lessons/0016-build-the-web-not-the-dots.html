Lesson 16 · Phase 4: Encoding

Build the Web, Not the Dots

From Dr. Justin Sung · "Learn to Learn" — Part 3 & "13 Years of No BS Study Advice"

Lower-order knowledge is isolated facts — definitions you recite. Higher-order knowledge is integrated: a concept matters because of its influence on other things — you see how components connect and interact ("if I change this, that goes down"). The memory difference is decisive: isolated facts decay fast; integrated knowledge forces itself into a schema / network — stronger memory, flatter decay.

The goalpost

When new information arrives, "put on your higher-order hat": how does this connect? what's the pattern? where does it fit? Any method works if it forces thinking in networks and relationships — not memorizing isolated facts.

Two moves that build webs

Create analogies — then critique them

Take related concepts, find how they connect, and re-represent them as something else; then test "does this analogy still make sense?" — that test forces you to examine whether you understood each piece correctly. Go further: critique your analogy. "Life is like a journey" → but life is less predictable → "a spontaneous journey" → through a foggy forest… Each critique forces deeper, more critical examination — higher cognitive load → deeper learning.

The Feynman method

Explain from memory in the simplest terms to an imaginary 10-year-old. It's higher-order (networks), generative (you produce it), and free recall (from memory) at once — you prioritize, restructure, and check accuracy.

Why do integrated concepts decay slower?
What makes analogy critique so powerful?

Recap: Higher-order learning = integrated knowledge in a network, which decays slower. Build webs with analogies (then critique them) and the Feynman method (explain to an imaginary 10-year-old).

Your win: You can build one web right now: take a concept you're learning, make an analogy, critique it once — then Feynman-explain it from memory.
