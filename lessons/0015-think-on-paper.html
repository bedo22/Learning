Lesson 15 · Phase 4: Encoding

Think on Paper

From Dr. Justin Sung · "If You Have A Bad Memory, I'll Help You Fix It"

Working memory's 15–30 seconds and ~5–9 item weight limit mean you can't hold seven complicated concepts in your head and find their connections at the same time. The fix — Justin Sung's favorite: use paper as a cognitive offload.

What offloading does

Write it down, draw it out. Now working memory doesn't waste resources holding info — it just looks at the paper and focuses on the one connection being made. This is the best use of note-taking for learning.

2025 evidence: the medium matters less than the thinking

The latest EEG evidence (256 electrodes, 36 students) found handwriting produces widespread theta/alpha brain connectivity in parietal and central regions — the signature of memory encoding — while typewriting produced little to none (Van der Weel & Van der Meer, 2024). But recent replications and meta-analyses moderate the hype: on actual quiz scores, laptop vs longhand note-takers perform equally (Urry et al., 2021), and the longhand advantage is small-to-moderate and shrinks when distraction is controlled (Flanigan et al., 2024; Voyer et al., 2022). The point is not the pen — it's that paper offloads so you can think. Transcription by any tool is the enemy.

Notes are for thinking, not copying

Notes are a cognitive offload for thinking, not the learning itself. You're not a human photocopier. The reference's rule: the better you learn, the less you need to write. Your notes should be a visual representation of your thought process — the connections you made — not a transcript of the source.

Handling six strategies, one practice

Think-on-paper is the partner of the other memory-handling strategies: you increase complexity by molding info on paper (extract keywords, simplify, re-explain in your own words, connect to prior knowledge), you handle it immediately by offloading within the window, and you prevent overload because the paper holds what working memory can't.
