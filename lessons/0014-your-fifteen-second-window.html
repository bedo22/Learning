Lesson 14 · Phase 4: Encoding

Your 15-Second Window

From Dr. Justin Sung · "If You Have A Bad Memory, I'll Help You Fix It"

You don't have a bad memory — you have poor memory handling. Here's the reality: information comes in, and you have a brief window to do something with it. Whether you handled it right decides whether it's stored — not the "quality" of your memory.

Working memory: the 15–30 second workbench

Working memory (short-term) lasts about 15–30 seconds and holds a weight limit of ~5–9 items. What you do in that window dictates how well the info is stored in long-term memory. Do nothing → gone in 1–2 minutes. Handle it right → forgotten only after days, weeks, or months.

How many items? It depends on your patterns

Classic theory says ~7 (Miller, 1956); modern studies land closer to ~4 chunks (Cowan, 2002). A 2024 modelling study reconciles the two: ~4 when you can't use patterns, ~6–7 once recoding and chunking kick in (Morra et al., 2024) — and a 2025 neural-model study shows chunking is learnable, so capacity behaves like a learnable skill, not a fixed anatomy (Soni & Frank, 2025). Practically: the more structure you can impose, the more your limited window holds.

The "weight limit" — why an item is fuzzy

~5–9 items, not words or digits. Patterns collapse items: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 is nine digits but trivially one item. Prior knowledge collapses items too: "serotonin syndrome" is two items to a layperson, one to a medic. Patterns and prior knowledge lighten the load.

What the window means for you

Handle it immediately

The flawed strategy: "consume everything first, make sense of it later." That assumes understanding = memory. It doesn't. Handle info within the 15–30 second window of first encountering it — ten extra seconds now saves the hour you'd spend relearning it later.

Pause for handling — prevent cognitive overload

If you keep consuming while trying to handle the previous piece, more piles in — you exceed the weight limit and hit cognitive overload, where the brain produces no effective output. Stop-start approach: one thing comes in → pause the video or stop reading → make meaning → then let the next piece through.
