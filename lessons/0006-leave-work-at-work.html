Lesson 06 · Phase 1: Rest & Plan

Leave Work at Work

From Dr. Justin Sung · "How to Rest So Well…" & "Productivity is Hard…"

It's 6pm. You're exhausted. You know a walk would help you recover. But instead, you collapse on the couch and scroll for two hours. Why? It's not laziness — it's prefrontal cortex fatigue, and the tiredness is lying to you.

The recovery paradox

Researchers found something brutal: people recover the least when they need it the most. When your workload is high, it's hardest to mentally detach from work — so the people most desperate for recovery are the least able to get it. The harder you work, the worse your rest becomes, and the cycle accelerates.

Why you can't "just relax"

As the day goes on, your prefrontal cortex — the part that makes decisions, plans, and regulates behavior — fatigues. A fatigued PFC can't make the high-effort decisions that recovery activities require. "Should I go for a walk or scroll?" is exactly the kind of decision a tired PFC can't make — so you default to the couch.

The tiredness is lying to you

"I'm too exhausted to do a recovery activity" is exactly backwards. The reason you can barely hold on is because you lack recovery activities. The effort feels impossible in the moment, but it's repaid with more energy. Don't trust the fatigued PFC's verdict.

Script your exit

The fix: pre-decide your end-of-day transition so your tired PFC only has to execute, not decide. This is a scripted action (if-then):

Example: "When I close my laptop at 6pm, I immediately put on my shoes and walk outside for 15 minutes." The walk is the transition — it gives you psychological detachment (one of the four recovery dimensions): you mentally switch off from work. By the time you're back, the work context has faded.

Take it further: the same trigger can open a play container instead of a screen: "When I close my laptop at 6pm → 15-minute walk, then 45 minutes from my play menu." The tired PFC only executes; the choice was already made on Sunday.

Recap: The recovery paradox means you rest least when you need it most. Your tired PFC can't decide to recover — so pre-decide an if-then exit ritual that lets you execute, not decide.

Your win: You have a scripted end-of-day transition (e.g., "close laptop → shoes on → 15-min walk") that doesn't rely on willpower or a decision your tired brain can't make.
