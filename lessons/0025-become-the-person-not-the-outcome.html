Become the Person, Not the Outcome

Reverse goal setting — why the goal fails when it's just an outcome, and the 3-step gap drill that fixes it

Normal goal setting asks "what do I want?" and then hopes. Reverse goal setting asks "who is the person for whom this goal would be easy?" — and works backward. Outcomes are symptoms of a process. This lesson gives you the drill: the meta-goal, the gap, and the one-or-two-things rule.

Why the outcome-first goal fails

An outcome is something you cannot directly control — the exam grade, the job offer, the money. A person is something you can build. When the goal is just an outcome, two things go wrong: the path is unclear (so you flail), and achieving it doesn't change who you are (so it doesn't stick — most lottery winners lose it all, because they have the money but not the person who keeps it). The science agrees: specific + challenging goals beat "do your best" (Locke & Latham), but only when they're backed by commitment and feedback — and goals that match your identity are pursued with sustained effort (Sheldon & Elliot, self-concordance). The outcome is the symptom; the person is the cause.

The drill: three steps

  1. Define the outcome you want — but keep it medium-term (≤3 years). Beyond that, plans get fragile: one broken link derails everything.
  2. Define the person. Ask: "who is the person that would find this goal easy? What skills, attributes, habits, and opportunities do they have?" Be detailed. This becomes your real goal — the meta-goal.
  3. Rate the gap. For each skill or habit from step 2: required /10, current /10. The gap between them is your plan. Big gap = high priority.

"I don't know" is a gap, not a dead end

Don't know what skills the goal requires? That's your first task: go learn the requirements. Don't know your current ability? That's self-awareness — start by observing and reflecting honestly. Both are fixable gaps, and fixing them is step one of the plan.

The bottleneck check

Before committing to the gap drill, check what's actually blocking you. A student once thought her blocker was learning skills — but she wasn't consistent, and digging deeper revealed she was being abused at home. You can't build skills without a foundation of stability and safety. The real bottleneck is often upstream of where you think: not learning skills but procrastination, not time but unwillingness to try. Step back and evaluate holistically before you invest in the wrong fix.
