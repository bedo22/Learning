Lesson 10 · Phase 2: Enablers

Procrastination is an Emotion

From Dr. Justin Sung · "Learn to Learn" — Part 4

You block Instagram, and thirty seconds later you're browsing the app store. That's not a distraction problem — it's an avoidance problem. Procrastination is a coping mechanism for the discomfort of anticipated effort: you pick an easier path to dodge the feeling. Block one distraction and you'll just find another.

The distractions aren't the problem

If it were about the feed, blocking the feed would fix it. It doesn't — because the task itself feels heavy, and avoiding that feeling is the real driver. Fix the discomfort, and the distractions stop mattering.

Short-term fixes (bridges, not cures)

  • Make both paths high-effort. When the productive path and the distraction path take the same effort, the choice is easy. Phone in another room, laptop closed — the feed is no longer the lazy option.
  • Accountability. A partner or group swaps fragile dopamine-based motivation for durable identity-based motivation: "I'm someone who keeps my word."
  • Use app blockers freely — but know they fade. They buy you time, not a cure.

The long-term fix: get good at being bored

That restless feeling when you sit still is the same feeling that derails your focus all day. Practice sitting through it: mindfully doing nothing, and each time the urge to grab your phone rises, just stay. Use the Zeigarnik effect to your advantage: when resistance hits, don't aim to finish — only start. Starting makes continuing far easier.

Why practice being bored?

Recap: Procrastination is emotional avoidance, not a distraction problem. Use high-effort paths and accountability as bridges — and train the long-term muscle: sit with the urge, practice boredom, and only aim to start.

Your win: You've named the feeling on your next real task and scripted one if-then decider: "If I feel the urge to avoid → I will do one small start for 2 minutes" — and one accountability or high-effort bridge you actually set up.
